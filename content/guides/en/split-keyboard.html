A split keyboard puts the left-hand keys and the right-hand keys in two groups, so your hands can sit apart at the width of your shoulders instead of squeezed together in front of you. Some split keyboards are one angled piece and others are two separate halves. Prices below are the ones the makers list.

What does a split keyboard do?

A split keyboard lets each hand type from a position closer to where your forearms already point. Most people's shoulders are wider than the hand position on a standard keyboard's home row, so typing on a flat board pulls the forearms inward and bends the wrists outward. That outward bend is called ulnar deviation, and Rempel (2008) is cited for the point that conventional layouts can force shoulder elevation, wrist ulnar deviation and head rotation. Splitting the keys lets you set the opening angle and the distance between the halves.

The idea is old. Wikipedia's article on ergonomic keyboards says Klockenberg proposed angled key halves in 1926 to keep the wrists straight, and Kroemer suggested in 1972 that an adjustable split keyboard may reduce user pain.

The evidence for health benefits is thinner than the geometry. OSHA recommends considering alternative keyboards for neutral wrist postures and shows a split design as an example, then states that available research does not provide conclusive evidence that these keyboards prevent discomfort and injury. Hedge of Cornell found evidence that they may be more comfortable than other keyboards, and a review by Fagarasanu and Kumar (2003) found they may only change the area of stress instead of eliminating it. A split keyboard is a comfort choice with a plausible mechanism and no promise about pain or injury. The ergonomic keyboard guide goes through what medical sources say.

What are tenting and negative tilt?

Tenting raises the inner edges of each half, so your palms face each other more than they face the desk, like the sides of a tent. Negative tilt lowers the back of the keyboard or raises the front, so the keys slope away from you. Tenting turns the hands toward a handshake position, which cuts the forearm twist a flat board asks for, and a front riser gives a neutral or negative slope so users do not have to bend the wrists upward, whereas standard keyboards tend to have a positive slope (Wikipedia).

OSHA's keyboard page warns that tilt from the rear legs or a steep design angle can bend the wrist upward and says not to use keyboard feet if they increase wrist bending. Some boards build the adjustments in: the Logitech ERGO K860 lists tilt legs at 0, -4 and -7 degrees, so its legs go negative.

What is the difference between fixed and fully split keyboards?

A fixed split joins the two halves in one body at a set angle. A fully split keyboard comes as two pieces that you place anywhere on the desk. The fully split type lets you set the width to your shoulders.

The Logitech ERGO K860 shows the fixed approach: a curved, split key frame with an integrated number pad and a pillowed wrist rest, at 149.99 dollars on Logitech's store. Kinesis lists the Freestyle2 as a fully split board sold with either 9-inch or 20-inch separation between halves, at 99 dollars for the PC wired model. Its keys are rubber dome membrane switches. The Freestyle Edge RGB+ at 219 dollars has the same two-piece layout with mechanical, hot-swap switches. The petitdoigt keyboard database classes the K860 as a unibody angled board, since both halves share one body.

Row-staggered or column-staggered: which split layout?

Split boards come in two key arrangements. A row-staggered split keeps the key rows offset to the right, as on a normal keyboard, so your touch-typing habits carry over. A column-staggered split moves each key column to match the length of the finger that presses it. The staggered vs ortholinear vs columnar comparison explains the three arrangements, and the pages on columnar keyboards and ortholinear keyboards cover each.

Row-staggered splits such as the Freestyle2 and the Dygma Raise 2 keep the standard letter positions under each finger. Column-staggered splits such as the Moonlander, Glove80 and Corne move the keys and give the thumbs several keys each, so Space, Backspace, Enter and modifiers can leave the pinky and the bottom row. You assign keys in firmware and move rarely used symbols to layers.

The finger placement guide shows which finger owns each key on a standard board. That map applies to a row-staggered split board without change. A column-staggered board keeps the same finger for each letter and moves the key under it.

Are there wireless split keyboards?

Yes, and the choice shapes the price and the desk. Kinesis sells the Advantage360 Professional with Bluetooth to five devices and a wireless link between the halves, powered by a 1,500 mAh battery. MoErgo lists Bluetooth to four hosts plus USB on the Glove80. Dygma sells the Raise 2 wired and adds wireless for 90 dollars; its wireless version carries a 2,370 mAh battery in each half. Many Corne, Lily58 and Sweep kits from Boardsource, Beekeeb and Typeractive offer Bluetooth. The ZSA Moonlander and Voyager are wired only, and Notebookcheck lists the missing wireless as a drawback of the Voyager.

A wireless split drops the cable between the halves and the cable to the computer, and each half carries its own battery. To see every wireless split in the petitdoigt keyboard database, type a description such as "split wireless keyboard under $300" into the keyboard finder.

Prebuilt or DIY: which split keyboard should I buy?

A prebuilt split keyboard ships ready to type and costs more than a kit. A kit sells a circuit board, a case and parts, and you solder or assemble the rest and add switches and keycaps. The Corne, an open design on GitHub, is the common DIY example, and each seller prices it differently.

What do reviewers say about split keyboards?

Reviewers praise comfort after an adjustment period and point to price, learning curve and compromises.

  • Logitech ERGO K860: RTINGS calls it a comfortable curved split board once you adapt, with weak raw performance for gaming. TechRadar rates it 4 out of 5 and points to the double-layer foam wrist rest.
  • ZSA Moonlander: RTINGS notes low, consistent latency, wired only and a learning curve from the layout.
  • ZSA Voyager: PC Gamer gave it 78 out of 100 as compact as a split board gets but too compromised to suit every user. Notebookcheck found it well built and highly customizable, with Choc V1 only, fragile keycaps and no wireless.
  • Kinesis Advantage360: Tom's Hardware gave it 4 out of 5, describing a comfortable contoured split board with deep programming, a steep learning curve, a high price and no hot-swap. Ben Frain called the wireless Pro comfortable with clunky configuration and occasional Bluetooth trouble.
  • MoErgo Glove80: RTINGS describes a wireless low-profile split with concave key wells, thumb clusters and tenting, customizable through ZMK and a web layout editor.
  • Dygma Raise 2: RTINGS calls it a true split with an aluminum plate, hot-swappable PCB, magnetic wrist rests and strong remapping software.
  • Microsoft Sculpt Ergonomic Keyboard: RTINGS found it a comfortable all-day office board with quiet typing, though the split layout takes adjustment and the keys feel mushy.

How do I prepare for a split keyboard?

A split keyboard keeps the finger assignments of a standard one, so the first step is knowing which finger belongs on which key. The touch typing guide covers technique, and petitdoigt's finger-coded drills in the browser show which finger presses each key on your layout.

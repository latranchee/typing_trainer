AZERTY and QWERTY share most of their keys. A French typist and an American one press E, R, T, Y, U, I, O and P with the same fingers, and S, D, F, G, H, J, K and L sit in the same places on the home row. Five letters move, the number row changes its job, and the punctuation at the bottom right shifts over. Those few keys are enough to trip a QWERTY typist on a French keyboard, and the reverse.

What is the difference between AZERTY and QWERTY, key by key?

French AZERTY swaps A with Q and Z with W, moves M to the home row right of L, and slides the bottom-right punctuation along to fill the spot M leaves. The table lists every key that differs between the layout Windows calls US and French (Legacy, AZERTY), the traditional layout Microsoft describes as ubiquitous in France. For letters, only four fingers change jobs. The left pinky still covers three letters in a new order: A on the top row, Q on its home key, W in the bottom-left corner. The left ring finger reaches up for Z where QWERTY has W. The right pinky now rests on a letter, M, instead of the semicolon, and the right index finger presses a comma where it used to press M. Every other letter stays under the same finger, so moving between AZERTY and QWERTY touches far fewer keys than a switch to Colemak or Dvorak.

The extra key beside the left Shift exists only on ISO keyboards, the shape used throughout Europe. ISO vs ANSI keyboards explains how to tell which one you have.

Why do you need Shift to type numbers on AZERTY?

AZERTY gives the unshifted number row to accented letters and punctuation, so the digits live on the Shift level. Pressing the 2 key alone types é; Shift with it types 2. Here is the whole row on the French Windows layout:

A few habits make the row easier to live with:

  • Press Shift with the opposite hand: right Shift for 1 to 5, left Shift for 6 to 0. The keyboard finger chart shows which hand owns each key.
  • Type long numbers on the numeric keypad, which gives digits without Shift.
  • On Windows, Caps Lock on the French layout works as a Shift lock for nearly the whole keyboard, so the number row types digits. Letters come out in capitals while it is on.
  • Watch the period. It is Shift plus the ; key, so every sentence ends with a Shift press, while ;, :, ! and ù need none.

How do you type accents and symbols on AZERTY?

Five accented letters have a key of their own and need no modifier: é, è, ç and à on the number row, ù beside M. Other accents come from dead keys, which print nothing until you type the next letter. Press ^ (right of P) and then e for ê; press Shift+^ and then i for ï. Programming symbols live on AltGr, the right Alt key: AltGr+à types @, AltGr+E types €, and the number row holds # { [ | \ ] } as the table above shows.

The ù key serves a single French word, où, while ê, which French text uses nearly four times as often as ù, takes two strokes. The traditional layout also cannot type everything French needs. Microsoft lists the ligatures æ and œ, French quotation marks « » and the capitals of some accented letters as missing. À, È and Ù can be reached through the grave dead key (AltGr+7, then Shift and the letter), but capital É and Ç have no key combination on the layout.

The 2019 standard and French (Standard, AZERTY)

The French Ministry of Culture's language delegation asked the standards body AFNOR to fix this in 2015. In 2019 AFNOR published NF Z71-300, a voluntary standard with an improved AZERTY alongside the BÉPO layout. It keeps the 26 letters and the digits where they are and moves some accents, punctuation and symbols so that É, Ç, æ, œ and « » become easy to type. Windows 11 version 24H2 ships it as French (Standard, AZERTY), where AltGr+Shift+é types É, and the old layout now appears as French (Legacy, AZERTY).

Check your keycaps before you pick the new one. Dell says only French (Legacy, AZERTY) matches the physical AZERTY keyboards on its laptops; with the Standard layout selected, some keys type characters other than the ones printed on them.

Why do France and Belgium use AZERTY?

France and Belgium use AZERTY because it spread early and stayed close to QWERTY, which helped it beat other French layouts. It appeared in France in the last decade of the 19th century as a variation on the QWERTY layout of American typewriters, and its exact origin is unknown. The ZHJAYS layout that Albert Navarre created in the early 20th century lost out, largely because AZERTY resembled QWERTY and was already popular.

Whoever designed it, the swaps fit French better than English. In the French letter counts Wikipedia reports, q makes up 1.36% of letters, against 0.095% in English, because of que, qui and quand; w barely exists, at 0.049% against 2.36% in English. AZERTY puts Q on a home key and sends W to the bottom-left corner. Z, at 0.33% of French letters against 0.074% in English, moves up from that corner to the top row. The letter é, at 1.50% of French letters, gets its own unshifted key.

Belgium has its own AZERTY. The letters sit in the same places and digits still need Shift, but several symbols move: the number row carries § and ! where the French layout has - and _, and = takes the bottom-right key where France has !. Apple's keyboards use the same AZERTY layout in France and Belgium, so on a Mac some symbols can sit in different places than on a Windows PC in the same country.

AZERTY is also used in some African countries. French-speaking Switzerland uses the Swiss French layout instead, a QWERTZ with é, è and à on their own keys (see QWERTZ vs QWERTY), and Canada has its own French layouts, covered below. The guide to keyboard layouts covers the wider family.

AZERTY vs QWERTY: which is better for typing, code and games?

Each layout is better at a different kind of text: AZERTY for French prose, QWERTY for English, numbers and code. We found no published study comparing typing speed on the two layouts, so treat claims that one is faster with suspicion. What you can compare is how many keystrokes each needs for the characters you type most.

Unless you touch type, you will glance at your keycaps, so the layout printed on the keyboards you use has a head start. Beyond that, count what you type: mostly English, numbers or code favors QWERTY, because brackets, braces and the backslash sit behind AltGr on AZERTY; mostly French favors AZERTY. Shortcuts follow the letters, so on AZERTY undo (Ctrl+Z) sits on the top row under the left ring finger and select-all (Ctrl+A) in the top-left corner.

Games move with the same four physical keys on both layouts, which AZERTY labels Z Q S D. A game that reads key positions works unchanged. A game that reads letters expects W, A, S and D, so you either rebind its controls or switch to a QWERTY layout while you play.

Is Canadian French a middle ground between AZERTY and QWERTY?

Yes, for anyone who writes French on QWERTY. The layout Windows calls Canadian French keeps every QWERTY letter in place and the digits unshifted, and fits French around them. The é has its own key at the bottom right, where US QWERTY has the slash, and Shift with it types a capital É, which French AZERTY cannot. The key right of P is a circumflex dead key and the next one a cedilla dead key for ç, while à, è and ù come from a grave-accent dead key where US QWERTY has the apostrophe. French quotation marks « » sit on the extra ISO key beside the left Shift.

It has costs too. The apostrophe needs Shift (it is Shift plus the comma key), and French uses it in every elision like l'été or c'est. The # sign takes the key left of 1. Since the letters do not move, a QWERTY typist can pick it up mostly by learning the accent keys. petitdoigt includes this layout, so you can practice Canadian French in the words drill.

How do you switch between AZERTY and QWERTY?

The operating system decides what each key types, so switching is a setting. On Windows 11:

  1. Open Settings, then Time & language, then Language & region.
  2. Select the three dots next to your language, then Language options.
  3. Under Keyboards, select Add a keyboard and pick French (Legacy, AZERTY), US or Canadian French. If the layout isn't listed, add the language it belongs to first.
  4. Hold the Windows key and press Space to cycle through the layouts you installed.

On a Mac, open System Settings, choose Keyboard, go to Text Input, click Edit and add the layout with the Add button. Control-Space returns to the last layout you used, and Control-Option-Space moves to the next one.

Windows also switches layouts with Alt+Shift or Ctrl+Shift, both easy to hit while typing. If your keyboard suddenly types Q when you press A, that is the likely cause: switch back with Windows key + Space, or remove the layout you don't use.

The keycaps stay the same whatever you choose. A US keyboard set to AZERTY types A when you press the key labeled Q, which is why the new positions are best learned by touch. How to type without looking at the keyboard walks through that.

How do you learn AZERTY, or go back to QWERTY?

Retrain the keys that moved and leave the rest alone. Most keys stay under the same finger, so the work falls on the left pinky, the left ring finger and the punctuation under the right hand.

  1. Settle on the new home row. On AZERTY it reads Q S D F and J K L M, with both pinkies resting on letters. The bumps on F and J stay where they were.
  2. Drill the moved letters inside real words. For AZERTY: que, quand, aussi, mais, comme, zéro. Going back to QWERTY: was, what, always, maybe, zone.
  3. Rebuild the end of a sentence: the comma under the right index, the period as Shift plus the next key over.
  4. Add the number row last, with Shift on the opposite hand.

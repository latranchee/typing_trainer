ISO, ANSI and JIS describe the physical keyboard: the size of each key and where it sits. The layout you type with, such as US QWERTY or German QWERTZ, is a software setting that runs on top of that shape (the guide to keyboard layouts covers those). Most of the board is identical on all three shapes. The differences sit at the edges, around Enter, the left Shift and the space bar, and they change what your pinkies do.

What is the difference between ISO and ANSI keyboards?

An ISO keyboard has a tall Enter that covers two rows, a short left Shift and one more character key than an ANSI keyboard, whose Enter fills a single row and whose left Shift runs all the way to Z. The extra ISO key sits between the left Shift and Z.

The tall Enter pushes one more key out of place. On ANSI, the backslash key (\ and | on a US board) ends the top letter row, right above Enter. ISO has no room for it there, so that key drops to the home row, just left of Enter; a British board prints # and ~ on it. The ISO/IEC 9995 keyboard standard counts 48 character keys on the ANSI arrangement and 49 on the ISO one. A full-size US PC keyboard has 104 keys and the usual European one has 105; without the number pad, a tenkeyless board drops to 87 keys on ANSI and 88 on ISO.

Keyboard makers measure keys in units: 1u is the width of one letter key. The table gives units where a source states them.

Which countries use ISO, ANSI or JIS?

The United States uses ANSI, Europe uses ISO almost everywhere, and Japan uses JIS. Apple's keyboard catalogue gives a precise list by language, since Apple files each of its language versions under one shape.

Korean boards come from the ANSI family and add two keys beside the space bar: 한/영 switches between Hangul and Latin letters, and 한자 converts Hangul to hanja. Some Asian markets also sell an ANSI variant with an extra-large Enter that fills both the ANSI and the ISO Enter spots. Sources split on the Netherlands. Apple sells a Dutch ISO keyboard, while the Deskthority wiki lists the Netherlands among the countries that use ANSI.

The shape follows the market a keyboard was built for. A laptop bought in London keeps its ISO board after you switch Windows or macOS to US English.

How do I tell if my keyboard is ANSI or ISO?

Look at the Enter key: a one-row Enter means ANSI, and a tall Enter that covers two rows means ISO or JIS. Four checks settle any doubt:

  1. Find Enter. If it fits in one row and the backslash key sits directly above it, you have ANSI.
  2. Look at the left Shift. If a single key separates it from Z, you have ISO. A British board labels that key \ |; a German one shows < > |.
  3. Look beside the space bar. Keys marked 無変換 and 変換 on either side of a short space bar, plus a ¥ key next to Backspace, mean JIS.
  4. Count the keys in the bottom letter row between the two Shifts. ANSI has 10, from Z to /. ISO and JIS both have 11, and Brazil's ABNT2 has 12.

On a Mac, macOS recognizes three keyboard types, ANSI, ISO and JIS, and opens Keyboard Setup Assistant when it can't identify a keyboard you plug in. When it gets the type wrong, two keys trade places, most often the key below Esc and the key to the right of the left Shift. Apple's guide for macOS Monterey has you open System Preferences, click Keyboard, then click Change Keyboard Type and follow the steps: the assistant asks you to press the key to the right of the left Shift, then lets you pick ANSI, ISO or JIS.

ISO vs ANSI: which is better for typing?

Neither shape has been shown to make you type faster: we found no study that compared typing speed on ISO and ANSI, and the articles that call one shape faster cite none. The two shapes differ in how far your pinkies travel to three keys:

  • Left Shift, left pinky. The ISO Shift is 1.25u against 2.25u on ANSI, and the extra key fills the gap. A pinky trained on ANSI that reaches short types \ or < instead of a capital letter. You notice it most on capitals typed with the right hand, such as H, J or M, because touch typists hold the left Shift for those.
  • Enter, right pinky. On ANSI, Enter comes straight after ' on the home row. ISO puts one more key between them, so the pinky travels farther along the home row or reaches up to the top half of the Enter.
  • Backslash, right pinky. ISO brings it down to the home row next to Enter, closer than its ANSI spot at the far end of the top row.

László Monda of Ultimate Hacking Keyboard compared the two shapes in key units and found Enter and the left Shift about one unit (he puts it at 20 mm) farther from the center of the board on ISO, with the backslash about one unit closer. He points out that people press Enter and the left Shift hundreds of times a day and the backslash far less often.

The slips people notice come from switching shapes. Coming from ANSI, the right pinky reaches for Enter on the home row and lands on the key left of the ISO Enter (# on a British board). Coming from ISO, a pinky that reaches up for the top half of Enter hits the ANSI backslash key instead. Both habits fade with slow, accurate repetitions on the board you mean to keep, eyes on the screen.

Do keyboard layouts work on both ISO and ANSI boards?

Yes: any layout runs on any shape, because the operating system decides which character each physical key types. The extra ISO key is the one catch, since some layouts put characters there that exist on no other key.

  • US layout on an ISO board. The Windows US layout gives the extra key a second \ |, and the home-row key left of Enter types the usual backslash. Nothing goes missing.
  • European layout on an ANSI board. The Windows German layout puts <, > and | only on the extra ISO key, and the French AZERTY and Spanish layouts put < and > there as well. An ANSI board has no such key, so those characters need a remap or another layout. The guides to QWERTZ vs QWERTY and AZERTY vs QWERTY cover the rest of those layouts.

European layouts also use the right Alt key as AltGr, which reaches a third character on many keys: on the British layout, AltGr with 4 types €. ISO boards usually print AltGr on that key. That matters for code. The German layout puts { [ ] } on AltGr with 7 to 0, while the US layout gives brackets their own keys right of P. Since a US layout loses nothing on an ISO board, a German coder can keep the ISO keyboard and switch layouts in software.

Dvorak, Colemak and Workman rearrange letters in the part of the board both shapes share, so they behave the same on ISO and ANSI. Colemak-DH is the exception: it uses the angle mod, which moves the keys along the bottom left one place to the left. On an ISO board the extra key absorbs the move; DreymaR's Big Bag of Keyboard Tricks calls it "practically a spare key". An ANSI board has no spare, so the common ANSI version moves Z to the middle of the bottom row. petitdoigt applies the layout itself from physical key positions, so you can try Colemak-DH in your browser without installing anything. The Colemak guide explains the rest of the layout.

What is a JIS keyboard?

JIS is the keyboard shape used in Japan: a tall ISO-style Enter plus extra keys for typing Japanese. A full-size JIS board has 109 keys, four more than ISO and five more than ANSI.

The extra keys take their room from shorter neighbors. The space bar shrinks to fit 無変換 (muhenkan) on its left and 変換 (henkan) and a kana key on its right: henkan converts typed kana to kanji, muhenkan leaves them unconverted, and the kana key switches between hiragana and katakana. Backspace shrinks to one unit to make room for a ¥ key, and the right Shift shrinks to fit a ろ (ro) key beside the slash. The left Shift stays long, so the bottom letter row holds 11 keys, as on ISO, with the extra key at the opposite end. The top-left key, which types ` on a US board, becomes 半角/全角 (hankaku/zenkaku), which toggles between half-width and full-width characters.

The letters themselves sit exactly where they do on ANSI and ISO, so a touch typist's home row works unchanged on a Japanese board. The reaches that change sit at the edges, around Backspace and the right Shift, and the thumbs work a narrower space bar.

Should you buy an ISO or ANSI keyboard?

Buy the shape your layout expects. If you type German, French, Spanish, British English or another European layout that puts characters on the extra key, get ISO. If you type US English or a US-based layout such as Dvorak, Colemak or Workman, ANSI gives you every key you need. Colemak-DH typists may still prefer ISO, where the angle mod gets its spare key.

If you already touch-type well on one shape, staying on it saves you from retraining Enter and the left Shift. Keycaps tip the balance for people who customize their keyboards: Corsair notes that keycap sets always come in ANSI but not always in ISO, and Keebsforall rates JIS keycap options as very limited.

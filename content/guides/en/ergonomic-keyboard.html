An ergonomic keyboard is shaped to let your hands and wrists rest in a straighter position than a standard board allows. The shape can come from a split key layout, raised inner edges, a downward slope, curved key wells or thumb keys. Evidence on how much that helps is thin, and prices run from 79 to 499 dollars.

What makes a keyboard ergonomic?

Wikipedia's article on ergonomic keyboards names the defining features, and each one targets a posture.

Most people's shoulders are wider than the home row of a standard board, so a flat board pulls the forearms inward and bends the wrists outward. Rempel (2008) is cited for the statement that conventional layouts can force shoulder elevation, wrist ulnar deviation and head rotation, and Rempel and colleagues published a study of split keyboard geometry and upper body postures in Ergonomics in 2009. The split keyboard guide covers tenting and the fixed and fully split types.

Wrist rests need care. OSHA says the forearms and wrists can be affected if a rest has sharp, hard leading edges and recommends choosing rests carefully. Wikipedia's carpal tunnel article says the evidence for using a wrist rest at a computer keyboard is debated. OSHA also warns that keyboard feet that raise the back of the board can bend the wrist upward, and tells readers not to use them if they increase wrist bending.

Is an ergonomic keyboard good for carpal tunnel and wrist pain?

Medical sources make no promise. The best controlled evidence on carpal tunnel syndrome is a Cochrane review published on 1 April 2022 by O'Connor and colleagues. It found two randomised or quasi-randomised trials with 105 people in total. In the smaller trial, 25 people, pain fell significantly at 12 weeks but not at six weeks, and hand function showed no difference between keyboards. In the larger trial, 80 people, three ergonomic keyboards compared with a standard one made no significant difference in pain severity at six months. The authors concluded that evidence is insufficient to say whether ergonomic positioning or equipment helps or harms people with carpal tunnel syndrome. That covers treatment of an existing condition, and says nothing about comfort.

OSHA's keyboard page suggests considering alternative keyboards for neutral wrist postures, shows a split design as an example and states that available research does not provide conclusive evidence that these keyboards prevent discomfort and injury. Wikipedia's article on ergonomic keyboards adds three findings: Hedge of Cornell found evidence that they may be more comfortable than other keyboards; Fagarasanu and Kumar (2003) found they may only change the area of stress instead of eliminating it; and the ACOEM guideline of Hegmann and colleagues (2016) noted indications that they are not well tolerated, because they break existing habits and often use more desk space. The article also says that injuries such as carpal tunnel and repetitive strain have many causes, and that an ergonomic keyboard alone does not make an ergonomic workplace.

On causes, Wikipedia's carpal tunnel article cites a 2023 systematic review by Lisica Mandek and Brborović that found studies of computer use and the condition inconclusive and contradictory, and says there is little or no data to support the idea that activity adjustment prevents carpal tunnel syndrome. If your hands hurt, tingle or go numb, a clinician can say what is happening. A keyboard change is a comfort experiment you can run alongside that advice.

Microsoft's ergonomic boards

The Sculpt Ergonomic Keyboard has a split, domed key bed and scissor-switch keys, runs on AAA batteries through a proprietary 2.4 GHz transceiver and comes with a separate wireless number pad. The data sheet lists 104-, 105- and 109-key configurations. RTINGS finds it comfortable for all-day office work with quiet typing, though the split layout takes adjustment, the keys feel mushy and the legends may fade. The Surface Ergonomic Keyboard adds Bluetooth and an Alcantara palm rest, and RTINGS calls it excellent and very quiet with outstanding build quality, Bluetooth only. Microsoft's own support page for the wired Ergonomic Keyboard describes a split, naturally arched key bed and a cushioned palm rest. Microsoft no longer lists the Surface or the wired Ergonomic Keyboard for sale, and the Sculpt is also out of production; Incase sells a licensed Sculpt.

Logitech ERGO K860

The K860 is an angled one-piece board. Logitech lists a curved, split key frame with an integrated number pad, a pillowed wrist rest, tilt legs at 0, -4 and -7 degrees, and two AAA batteries rated up to 24 months. RTINGS calls it comfortable once you adapt, with weak raw gaming performance. TechRadar scored it 4 out of 5 and noted the double-layer foam wrist rest. Its keys are scissor switches.

Split and key-well boards

Kinesis, ZSA, MoErgo and Dygma make the specialist boards. The Kinesis Advantage2 has two concave key wells tented at a fixed angle and uses Cherry MX Brown or Quiet Red switches, and Kinesis sells the Advantage360 with the wells split into two halves. Tom's Hardware calls the Advantage360 comfortable with deep programming but a steep learning curve, a high price and no hot-swap. The ZSA Moonlander and MoErgo Glove80 add thumb clusters, and the columnar keyboard guide and ortholinear keyboard guide explain their key arrangement. The staggered vs ortholinear vs columnar page compares all three arrangements side by side.

Which is the best ergonomic keyboard?

The best ergonomic keyboard depends on the wrist posture you want to change, the desk space you have and how much you are willing to relearn. Four factors narrow the choice.

  • Budget: the listed prices above run from 79 dollars to 499 dollars.
  • Typing feel: scissor boards such as the K860 and the Sculpt are quiet, mechanical boards let you choose switches, and the best switches for typing and low-profile switches guides cover the options.
  • Learning cost: a row-staggered split keeps letters where your fingers expect them, while column stagger and thumb clusters move keys.
  • Connection: the K860, the Advantage360 Professional and the Glove80 offer wireless, while the Moonlander is wired.

The reviews quoted above (RTINGS on the K860 and the Sculpt, Tom's Hardware on the Advantage360, TechRadar on the K860) include measurements and more detail on each board. What does an ergonomic keyboard not change?

Typing technique comes from practice on any board: staying on the home row, using the right finger for each key and looking at the screen. The keyboard finger chart shows which finger owns each key on a standard layout, and the touch typing guide covers the technique.

petitdoigt, a free typing trainer in the browser, draws a finger-coded keyboard for your layout and board shape and records timing per key and per finger. It gives no medical advice and makes no claim about wrist pain.

Colemak and Dvorak both move the most common English letters under your resting fingers, and both score far better than QWERTY on two common measures of comfort: the share of typing done on the home row, and how often one finger has to hit two different keys in a row. They part ways on how much of QWERTY survives. Dvorak, patented in 1936, leaves only two letters, A and M, where QWERTY has them. Colemak, released in 2006, moves 17 keys and keeps the undo, cut, copy and paste shortcuts where your left hand expects them. Workman, from 2010, moves 21. For national layouts such as AZERTY and QWERTZ, see the overview of keyboard layouts.

How do Colemak and Dvorak differ in design?

Dvorak is built around alternating hands, and Colemak around keeping QWERTY habits. August Dvorak and William Dealey filed their patent in May 1932 and received it in May 1936. Their layout puts every vowel, Y included, under the left hand and the most used consonants under the right, so most English words bounce from one hand to the other. The left home row reads A O E U I and the right one D H T N S. The guide to the Dvorak keyboard covers its history and the studies people still argue about.

All that alternation comes at a price. Colemak's FAQ says Dvorak buys it with a higher same-finger rate, a load that tilts toward the right hand, extra strain on the right pinky (L sits where QWERTY has P) and fewer hand rolls, where one hand types several keys in one smooth motion. Workman's author puts same-hand use in the 30% range for most layouts and in the 20s for Dvorak. The Colemak FAQ also points out that I, one of the most frequent letters, sits off the home position on Dvorak, at a sideways stretch of the left index.

Shai Coleman released Colemak on 1 January 2006 to keep as much of QWERTY as possible and move a letter only where it pays off. Seventeen keys move, and only two of them, P and E, switch hands. The home row becomes A R S T D H N E I O, so words like "strand" and "senior" never leave it. By default Colemak also turns Caps Lock into a second Backspace. The Colemak guide walks through learning it.

Colemak's weak spot is the middle of its home row. D and H sit in the two center columns, so the index fingers reach sideways for them, and a common pair such as HE forces a sideways twist of the right hand. Colemak-DH and Workman were both made to remove that reach.

Which is easier to learn, Colemak or Dvorak?

Colemak is easier to learn for anyone who types QWERTY today. Fewer keys move, only two change hands, and Ctrl+Z, X, C and V stay put, so your shortcuts keep working while you relearn the letters. Almost all punctuation stays in its QWERTY spot as well. Dvorak moves the comma and period to the top row, the semicolon to the bottom left and the brackets up to the number row, which is more to relearn if you write code or punctuation-heavy text.

Colemak was built on that argument: its FAQ calls Dvorak "too difficult and frustrating to learn for existing QWERTY typists". Read that as the layout's own site making its case. We found no controlled study of how long either layout takes to learn. Colemak's FAQ says the time depends on the person and goes mostly into "improving the motor skills and finger dexterity involved in typing".

Colemak also offers a gentler path. According to Wikipedia's Colemak article, the Tarmak transitional layouts change only three to five keys at a time over five steps, so most of the keyboard stays familiar at every stage.

Is Colemak or Dvorak faster than QWERTY?

Neither layout has been shown to make you type faster than QWERTY. The Dvorak vs QWERTY question has the longest paper trail. During the Second World War, while in the Navy, Dvorak ran experiments that he said retrained typists to his layout in ten days; he had discarded at least two earlier studies whose results are unknown. In 1956, Earle Strong of the US General Services Administration ran a controlled study with ten typists per group that, in Wikipedia's words, "failed to show any benefit to the Dvorak keyboard layout in typing or training speed". Strong blamed Dvorak's earlier results on poor experimental design and bias, though he had a personal grudge against Dvorak himself. In 1990, S. J. Liebowitz and Stephen Margolis reviewed the record in "The Fable of the Keys" and found that the best-documented experiments "suggest little or no advantage for the Dvorak keyboard".

Dvorak's best-known speed record comes from a single typist. Barbara Blackburn, who typed Dvorak from 1938, appeared in the Guinness Book of World Records from 1976 to 1985 at 150 words per minute held for 50 minutes. Guinness listed her without an official test, after a Dvorak advocate wrote to the editors, and one typist's record cannot separate the layout from the person.

The Colemak vs QWERTY record is thinner still, since we found no controlled speed study of Colemak. Its case rests on simulations like the ones in the table, which measure comfort: how far fingers reach, how much typing stays on the home row, how often one finger works twice in a row. Those numbers matter for strain and for how typing feels. A switch also costs you speed for a while before it can pay anything back, so if speed is your goal, start with how to type faster on the layout you already have, and compare yourself against average typing speeds.

Where do Workman and Colemak-DH fit?

Workman and Colemak-DH both target Colemak's sideways reach to D and H, and they pay for it in different ways. OJ Bucao published the Workman layout in September 2010 after grading every key from 1 (easiest) to 5 (most strenuous). His aim was to keep common letters out of the two middle columns, which by his count take 22% of keystrokes on QWERTY, 14% on Dvorak, 12% on Colemak and 6% on Workman. The cost is same-finger pairs: 3.15% in the Mod-DH comparison against Colemak's 1.67%. Bucao's own figures point the same way, with a same-finger pair every 46 keystrokes on Workman, every 58 on Colemak and every 20 on QWERTY.

Colemak-DH keeps Colemak and moves only what causes the reach. SteveP released it in October 2014. D drops to the bottom row under the left index, G returns to its QWERTY key, B moves up to the top row and H trades places with M, all without changing which finger presses any key. Versions for staggered keyboards can add an angle mod that shifts X and C (and Z, on ISO boards) one key to the left for a more symmetrical posture, though the project now recommends its original matrix version on every keyboard type. Colemak-DH keeps Colemak's 1.67% same-finger rate and lowers the effort score from 1.802 to 1.735 in the same keyboard configuration; on matrix keyboards it scores 1.726, the lowest in the comparison. Linux includes it in xkeyboard-config 2.34 and later, and the project provides files for macOS and Windows. In petitdoigt you can drill Colemak-DH letter pairs without installing anything.

What happens to Ctrl+C and Ctrl+V on each layout?

Shortcuts follow the letters in most programs, so a layout that moves C and V moves copy and paste with them. Colemak keeps Z, X, C and V exactly where QWERTY has them. Workman keeps Z and X and shifts C and V one key to the right, still within reach of the left hand alone. Colemak-DH depends on the version: the matrix version keeps Z, X and C in place and moves V one key right, and the angle-mod versions shift X and C one key left and leave V in place. Dvorak scatters them: X lands under the left index, while C, V and Z move to the right hand, so undo, copy and paste need the hand that usually holds the mouse.

Dvorak users have workarounds on both major desktop systems. macOS includes a "Dvorak – QWERTY ⌘" layout that turns into QWERTY while you hold Command, and Windows users get the same effect with AutoHotkey scripts.

Which layout should you pick?

Pick Colemak if you type QWERTY today and want the easiest switch with the best measured comfort. Stay with Dvorak if you already type it; Colemak's FAQ gives the same advice to anyone generally happy with Dvorak. The table covers the other cases.

How can you try Colemak or Dvorak before you switch?

You can try all three in a browser tab without touching your system settings. petitdoigt, a free trainer that runs in the browser, applies the layout itself from the physical position of each key, so Colemak, Dvorak and Workman work on the QWERTY keyboard already on your desk. The on-screen keyboard shows the new layout's letters and colors each key by the finger that should press it; the keyboard finger chart explains those finger zones. The browser cannot see which finger you used: it highlights the right one and measures your keys and timing.

  1. Type a few minutes of the words drill on each layout you are considering, slowly, following the highlighted finger.
  2. Take the one that felt least awkward to the pairs drill. In adaptive mode it builds each set mostly from your own slowest and most error-prone key-to-key transitions, so it finds that layout's hard pairs for your hands.
  3. Open the stats screen. It keeps separate numbers for each layout, so your Colemak practice never mixes with your QWERTY history.
  4. Once the new layout feels worth the effort, add it to your system. On Windows 11 version 24H2 or later, go to Settings, Time & Language, Language & region, open the options for English and add a keyboard. macOS and Linux list Colemak and Dvorak among their built-in layouts.

A Topre keyboard uses switches that sense a keypress electrically without any metal touching metal. Topre Corporation, a Japanese industrial company, makes them, and they are best known from its own Realforce keyboards and from PFU's Happy Hacking Keyboard (HHKB). The company calls the technology electrostatic capacitive; enthusiasts shorten it to Topre or EC. For the other families of switches, the guide to keyboard switches gives the overview.

How does a Topre switch work?

A Topre switch stacks four parts: a slider in a housing, a rubber dome, a conical coil spring and the circuit board. Pressing the key pushes the slider onto the dome, the dome collapses over the spring, and a capacitive sensor on the board notices the spring moving closer. The dome supplies most of the resistance and the tactile feel, while the spring acts as an electrode and contributes only about 5 cN of the actuation force.

The sensor registers the press partway down the stroke, and that point cannot be felt. The tactile bump you do feel comes near the top of the stroke. Because nothing makes contact, the design supports N-key rollover: every key you hold down registers at once. Realforce rates its current switches for 100 million keystrokes, and its older R2 pages gave 50 million.

The design goes back to the late 1970s, when a young engineer took the shape of the conical spring from the Japanese mosquito coil. The company entered electronics in 1976 with keyboards for computer terminals and sold to US customers from 1981. The patent is US 4,584,444, filed in 1984. Topre's electronic equipment division makes the switches, and the Realforce brand launched in 2001.

Contact switches such as Cherry's register a press when metal touches metal; the Topre switch has no contacts and no click mechanism. The guide to linear, tactile and clicky switches explains those three feels and where Topre fits: it counts as tactile.

What weights do Topre switches come in?

Topre switches come in 30, 35, 45 and 55 g. Deskthority prints these as cN, close to gram-force; Realforce's own pages use grams. The petitdoigt switch database holds 13 Topre switches, the 45 g and the silent 30 g among them, and 15 more capacitive switches from NIZ and Varmilo.

Cherry-style boards give every key in a set the same spring, while variable weighting changes it by finger. Other makers' boards sometimes use differently weighted domes for the same reason. The guide to switch specs explains how to read force figures like these.

Current Realforce boards add a setting Topre calls APC, short for Actuation Point Changer. It sets the depth at which each key registers: 0.8 to 3.0 mm on the R4, four steps (0.8, 1.5, 2.2 and 3.0 mm) on the RC1 and R3, and 0.1 mm steps from 0.1 to 3.0 mm on the GX1 and GX1 Plus, which also have a rapid-trigger-style release mode. Total travel is 4 mm on the standard switch, and the HHKB Type-S silent version lists 3.8 mm.

Which keyboards use Topre switches?

Two brands carry most of the Topre range: Realforce, made by Topre itself, and the HHKB from PFU, a Ricoh company. Both brand pages say the boards are made in Japan. The petitdoigt keyboard database lists 15 Topre boards from the two brands, 12 of them still sold.

Most Topre boards use a row-staggered layout, so the finger reaches match a standard keyboard. The finger placement chart shows the standard positions; the 60-key HHKB has fewer keys than that chart. Realforce's website lists Mechanical Keyboards, LLC as its US shop.

Are Topre switches quiet?

Topre sells silent versions. Most of Realforce's current line offers only silent 30 g or 45 g switches. The HHKB Type-S is the silent HHKB. The guide to quiet switches covers silent switches from other makers.

What are the alternatives to Topre: NIZ, Varmilo and MX-stem boards?

Several brands build capacitive switches that borrow the Topre idea in a different housing. They are separate designs, and each fits only its own boards.

  • NIZ. NIZ's electrocapacitive switch has a Cherry MX-style stem and slider inside a round, Topre-like housing, with an O-ring that damps the upstroke. The 35 g and 45 g versions differ only in springs. NIZ sells add-on springs (10 g and 20 g) and silicone domes (35, 45, 50 and 55 g) to change the weight, and says the Atom 68's trigger point can switch between 2 mm and 3 mm.
  • Varmilo EC. Varmilo's electrostatic capacitive switches use a spring and plastic slider like a Cherry switch, with capacitance sensing in place of metal contacts. They come as V2 and V3 (hot-swap, from October 2024) in linear, tactile and clicky feels, and fit Varmilo EC boards only. Tom's Hardware gives 60 g at bottom-out for the Sakura.
  • Cherry MX-stem Topre. Topre's own cross-stem switch went into the Realforce RGB, now discontinued, and Deskthority calls the Cooler Master NovaTouch TKL the first Topre-made switch compatible with Cherry MX keycaps.

Force, feel and keycap mount decide between Topre and a mechanical board. The guide to the best switches for typing lists what to weigh, and the guide to who makes switches links each maker's official site, Topre and NIZ included.

Is a Topre keyboard good for touch typing?

Topre keyboards use standard key positions and a row-staggered layout on most models, so touch typing works as on any board: each finger keeps its keys. Weight is the variable you can match to your hands: lighter switches need less force to register, and Realforce lets you set the actuation depth.

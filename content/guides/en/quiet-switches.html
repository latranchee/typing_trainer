A silent keyboard uses switches with dampers and a body that does not amplify them. Decibel figures without a test method say little about the keyboard on your desk, so none appear here. For the loud end of the range, see clicky switches; silent switches are one branch of keyboard switches.

What are silent switches, and how do they work?

A silent switch is an ordinary linear or tactile switch with a damper added. A mechanical switch makes noise in two places: the stem hits the bottom of the housing when you press the key all the way down, and it hits the top when the spring pushes it back. Silent versions put their dampers where the stem meets the housing.

Makers do this in different ways:

  • Cherry's MX Silent Red, as Deskthority describes it, has a slider with an integral, fully molded-in damper. Cherry's own pages list the MX2A Silent Red at 45 cN with 3.7 mm total travel, 0.3 mm less than the 4 mm of a regular MX2A Red.
  • Gateron's KS-9 silent set has silent pads above and below the stem, and its KS-33 low-profile silent switch has silent rings on both sides of the stem. The Zero Degree has mute pads at the stem guide and the bottom.
  • Razer says its Yellow switch has a built-in sound dampener, with actuation and reset both at 1.2 mm.
  • Keychron's Silent Banana is a silent tactile switch listed at 55 gf with 3.3 mm total travel.

Silent switches often list a shorter total travel, 3.3 to 3.8 mm in the examples above against 4 mm for a regular MX2A Red. The guide to switch specs explains forces and travel figures. The petitdoigt switch database holds 143 silent switches from 26 makers, 90 linear and 53 tactile, and Gateron makes 40 of them.

What makes a keyboard loud besides the switch?

The switch is one of several sources. Das Keyboard and Wooting, two keyboard makers who wrote guides on the subject, name these:

  • Bottoming out. Das Keyboard says keycaps clack when they bottom out.
  • Keycaps. Wooting says thin keycaps give a higher-pitched click and thick ones a lower clack that many find more pleasing.
  • Plate. Wooting writes that a plate affects how sound travels, and extra plate screws can make the sound less uniform.
  • Case. Wooting calls a hollow space inside the keyboard an echo chamber and says thin plastic cases that flex are worse than heavy aluminum. Das Keyboard says a case with sound-dampening foam is quieter than one without.
  • Desk. A hard desk carries the vibration: Das Keyboard names metal and wooden desks, and Wooting says a hollow wooden table picks up the vibrations. Both suggest a desk mat.

Das Keyboard also lists lubed switches and stabilizers and O-rings on the keycap stems as quiet-down steps. Thinner O-rings keep more feel and more noise, and thicker ones dampen more and can feel sloppy. Wooting rates O-rings by Shore hardness and says a higher number means harder and more silent.

These sources describe pitch as well as loudness: Wooting notes that a lower-pitched sound can be preferred even at equal volume.

Do silent switches feel different, and are they worth it?

The damper sits where the stem lands, so the bottom of the keypress differs from a regular switch. Typists who like a firm landing should try a silent switch before committing to a whole board of them.

Reviews of individual switches vary. ThereminGoat finds Cherry's MX Northern Light silent linear smooth and barely changing after break-in, though less evenly lubed than rival silent linears. It finds the MX2A Honey strong in its bump but with scratch, weak dampening and loud housing noise. The silent label states the maker's aim, and the result depends on the switch and the board around it.

Silent switches suit a shared room or a call-heavy day. Plain linear and tactile switches can be made quieter through lube and O-rings, and Das Keyboard says as much. The linear, tactile and clicky kinds are compared in their own guide.

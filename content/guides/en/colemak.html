What is the Colemak keyboard layout?

Colemak is an alternative to QWERTY built for touch typing in English. Shai Coleman released it on 1 January 2006 with two goals that pull against each other: keep your fingers on the home row most of the time, and change little enough that a QWERTY typist can make the move without starting from zero. The official layout also types accented letters for more than 40 languages, such as the â in pâté and the ñ in mañana, plus symbols like €.

The home row carries A R S T D H N E I O. By Peter Norvig's English letter counts, those ten keys hold the nine most frequent letters (E, T, A, O, I, N, S, R and H) and add up to about 74% of the letters you type. QWERTY's home row holds three of those nine letters and about 32% of typed letters, so your fingers spend far more time reaching up and down.

Each finger rests on the same physical key as on QWERTY, so the rules in the keyboard finger chart still apply. On the left hand, the pinky rests on A, the ring finger on R, the middle finger on S and the index on T, with D one key to its right. On the right hand, the index rests on N with H one key to its left, the middle finger on E, the ring finger on I and the pinky on O.

What changes from QWERTY?

Colemak changes 17 keys, all on the three letter rows: eight on the top row, eight on the home row and one on the bottom row. The number row, Shift, Ctrl, Alt and almost all punctuation stay where QWERTY puts them.

Z, X, C and V stay on purpose. Ctrl+Z, X, C and V remain one-hand shortcuts for the left hand while the right hand holds the mouse. Q and W stay as well: Colemak's design notes point out that Ctrl+Q (quit) and Ctrl+W (close window) can throw away work if they land somewhere you hit by accident.

Only two letters change hands, E and P. E moves from the left middle finger to the right middle finger, on the key QWERTY labels K. P moves from the right pinky to the key where QWERTY has R, under the left index finger. The semicolon leaves the home row for the top-right letter key, and the official layout turns Caps Lock into a second Backspace. The small number of hand changes is deliberate. The Colemak FAQ calls Dvorak too difficult and frustrating for existing QWERTY typists to learn, and colemak.com lists "only 2 keys move between hands" as a reason Colemak is easy to pick up.

Is Colemak faster or better than QWERTY?

Colemak beats QWERTY by a wide margin on the measures layout designers use, such as home-row use and same-finger bigrams. Its effect on typing speed is unproven, and even Colemak's community site says speed gains are hard to prove. A same-finger bigram is two letters in a row typed by one finger, which has to finish the first key before it can start the next. On QWERTY, the common ending "ed" goes to the left middle finger twice. On Colemak, E sits under the right middle finger and D under the left index, so the two letters go to different hands.

The home-row column adds up Norvig's letter frequencies for the letters on each layout's home row; Wikipedia's Colemak article gives the same 74%, 70% and 32% for Colemak, Dvorak and QWERTY. The same-finger column comes from the Colemak Mod-DH project's comparison page, which ran a set of Project Gutenberg books through its own effort model. Workman's own site puts Workman at 2.185%, so read the column as a design estimate from people who favor Colemak. Colemak's own site claims your fingers travel 2.2 times as far on QWERTY.

The same community site tells newcomers: "Don't learn Colemak for speed alone. Learn it for comfort, and for fun." Erez Zukerman, CEO of the keyboard maker ZSA, wrote that Colemak "did not make me a faster typist." He types 80 to 90 words per minute on it at 97 to 98% accuracy and values it for comfort, with less stretching and reaching. If speed is your main goal, technique and accuracy on the layout you already use pay off sooner, as the guide on how to type faster explains. For a side-by-side with the other alternatives, see Colemak vs Dvorak vs Workman.

What is Colemak-DH, and should you pick it?

Colemak-DH (full name Colemak Mod-DH) is a community variant that moves D and H out of the two centre columns, where plain Colemak puts them and where your index fingers have to slide sideways. SteveP launched it in October 2014. D and H drop to the bottom row, where the index fingers curl down to reach them, G and M take their places on the home row, and B moves up to the top row. According to the project, keystrokes on centre-column keys fall from 14.78% on Colemak to 7.80% on Colemak-DH.

The left half of the bottom row also uses the "angle mod": Z, X and C shift one key to the left while keeping their fingers, and D slides in under the left index. On an ISO board, Z takes the extra key beside the left Shift. An ANSI board has no such key, so Z goes to the middle of the row, as in the table. If you don't know which board you have, the guide on ISO vs ANSI keyboards shows how to tell.

From May 2017 to October 2020 the project recommended a second version, DHk, with K on the home row for standard staggered keyboards. It has since gone back to the original, sometimes called DHm, and recommends it on every keyboard. That is the version with M on the home row shown above.

Colemak's community site rates both as good and leaves the choice to preference. Pick Colemak-DH if sliding sideways to D and H feels awkward, or if you type on an ortholinear (matrix) keyboard, the setup where the Mod-DH comparison gives Colemak-DH its best score. Pick plain Colemak if you want the fewest changes and a layout your system already includes. If you already type plain Colemak comfortably, moving to DH means relearning the changed keys, which pays off mainly if the centre-column reach bothers you.

How long does it take to learn Colemak?

Plan on about two months of short daily practice before Colemak is fast enough to use full time. The figures that exist come from the layout's guidance and from users' reports. The official learning guide recommends 10 minutes a day for a couple of months, switching for good once you reach two thirds of your old QWERTY speed, and repeating each lesson until you hit at least 97% accuracy without hesitating. It also warns that the first 10 days are hard, especially if you switch all at once.

Tarmak, made by the Colemak community member DreymaR, starts with the most essential moves: step one fixes E and N, step two brings T into place, and a separate Tarmak-DH path ends at Colemak-DH. Erez Zukerman learned this way. He started with four keys changed and used that near-QWERTY layout for about a month before changing more, which kept him from hitting what he called "a wall."

Your own pace depends on hours of practice and on whether QWERTY still fills your workday. The Colemak FAQ says most of the learning time goes into motor skills, and that someone who already touch types quickly and correctly in QWERTY won't take long to regain that speed. Only the moved keys need relearning: 17 on Colemak, 19 on Colemak-DH plus the angle mod's three. If you look at the keys today, learning to touch type comes first, and you can learn it directly on Colemak.

How to learn Colemak, step by step

  1. Pick Colemak or Colemak-DH, and check your board. For Colemak-DH the bottom-left keys differ between ISO and ANSI boards, so know which one sits on your desk.
  2. Try it in the browser before you change any setting. petitdoigt applies the layout itself from the physical key positions, so you can type Colemak in your browser without installing anything in your operating system. The on-screen keyboard shows the Colemak letters, each key in its finger's color, so your keycaps can keep their QWERTY labels.
  3. Learn the home row finger by finger. Left hand A R S T, right hand N E I O, with D and H as the index fingers' inward reach (G and M on Colemak-DH). The anchors drill sends chosen fingers out to their keys and back to the home row between taps. The trainer highlights the finger to use, but it cannot see your hands, so keeping the right finger on each key is up to you.
  4. Add the top and bottom rows in real words. Whole words train the new positions in the order you will use them, including E and P, the two letters that changed hands.
  5. Drill the transitions that slow you down. The pairs drill in adaptive mode measures each key-to-key transition as you type and builds the next set mostly from your slowest and least accurate ones. Stats are kept per layout, so your Colemak numbers stay apart from your QWERTY history.
  6. Hold accuracy before you chase speed. Aim for 97% before you move on, the figure both the Colemak learning guide and the Tarmak FAQ give. The guide on typing accuracy explains why accuracy comes first.
  7. Switch your system layout at two thirds of your old speed. If you still share QWERTY machines, keep 5 to 10 minutes of QWERTY a day so it stays usable.

How do you turn on Colemak in Windows, macOS and Linux?

Colemak is built into Windows 11 from version 24H2 and comes pre-installed on macOS and Linux. Microsoft's list of Windows keyboard layouts shows Colemak as a Windows 11 addition.

  • Windows 11: Start, Settings, Time & language, Language & region. Click the three dots next to your language, choose Language options, then Add a keyboard and pick Colemak. Windows+Space cycles through your installed layouts.
  • macOS: System Settings, Keyboard, then Edit under Text Input. Click the add button, search for English and add Colemak. Control+Space switches back to the last input source. To make Caps Lock a Backspace as on the official layout, colemak.com suggests Karabiner-Elements.
  • Colemak-DH: the Mod-DH project provides Windows installers and an AutoHotkey script, and a macOS bundle (also brew install --cask colemak-dh). On Linux, xkeyboard-config 2.34 and later include it: setxkbmap us -variant colemak_dh, or setxkbmap uk -variant colemak_dh_iso on a UK ISO board.

Older Windows versions need the installer from colemak.com. For the national layouts and the other alternatives, the overview of keyboard layouts shows how they compare.

What are the downsides of Colemak?

The first cost is time: the learning guide warns that the first 10 days are hard and plans on a couple of months of practice. After that, the friction comes from computers you don't control and from software that binds keys by letter. Shared and work machines still run QWERTY, so you either keep QWERTY in shape or install Colemak wherever you type.

Colemak keeps the most used shortcuts and moves the rest with their letters. Ctrl+Z, X, C, V, Q and W stay in place. Ctrl+S (save) moves to the key QWERTY calls D, and Ctrl+F (find) to the key QWERTY calls E.

Games that bind controls by letter rather than by key position scatter the WASD cluster: W and A stay, while S and D land on the QWERTY D and G keys. Rebind the controls in the game, or switch to QWERTY with the system shortcut while you play. Vim has the same issue with H, J, K and L. H stays, but J moves to the QWERTY Y key, K to N and L to U, so Vim users either relearn those motions or remap them.

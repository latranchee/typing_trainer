The difference between a mechanical vs membrane keyboard sits under each key. A mechanical keyboard has a separate switch for every key, with its own housing, spring and contacts. A membrane keyboard uses a sheet of rubber domes over layered plastic membranes, and pressing a key squashes a dome until the membranes touch and close the circuit. How the two feel, how loud they are, how long they are rated to last and what they cost all follow from that construction.

What is the difference between a mechanical and a membrane keyboard?

RTINGS defines a mechanical keyboard as one with "individual mechanical switches under each key" that bring metal contacts together; it groups optical and magnetic switches with them. A membrane keyboard uses "a flexible, pressure-sensitive membrane layer": the key pushes a rubber dome or conductive pad through the layers to complete the circuit. Wikipedia describes the common membrane as three layers, two carrying the circuit traces with a spacer between them, and calls full-travel rubber dome over membrane "the most common keyboard design manufactured today."

How do rubber dome, membrane and scissor keyboards work?

All three use a rubber dome as the spring and, in most designs, a membrane as the switch. The Deskthority wiki describes the domes as "collapsible rubber domes to provide resistance and tactility," either separate pieces or "a single sheet of rubber," and says most rubber dome keyboards put "a membrane contact sheet below the dome." When you press, "the 'ceiling' of the dome presses two plastic membranes together to connect circuit traces." Some designs coat the inside of the dome with carbon so the dome itself closes the circuit.

The parts above the dome vary. In the most common version, a slider moulded into the keycap pushes on the dome. Scissor keyboards add two interlocking plastic pieces under each key; Deskthority says "laptop keyboards introduced the use of scissor-like mechanisms to stabilise low travel switches," and Wikipedia describes scissor boards as generally quiet, needing little force and "typically slightly more expensive." Apple's Magic Keyboard, Logitech's MX Keys S and the Keychron B33 use scissor keys.

Two designs sit between the families. Topre switches use a rubber dome with a conical spring below it, and sense the press by capacitance; Deskthority notes that Realforce boards "use the rubber dome solely as a spring." The guide to Topre switches covers them. At the budget end, some membrane boards borrow mechanical traits. MSI says its VIGOR GK30 uses plunger switches, a membrane design with a mechanical-like feel, and Cherry Xtrfy calls the membrane-based switches of its K33 Compact Wireless "Mem-chanical." The Glorious GMBK 75 puts MX-compatible stems on rubber domes, so mechanical keycaps fit.

Are membrane keyboards linear or tactile?

They are softly tactile: the dome resists, then collapses, and the collapse gives a vague bump. RTINGS says membrane keyboards "don't have different switch types like mechanical switches" and that the domes "can feel vaguely tactile but lack precision and consistency." Mechanical boards split into three switch families: linear, with a smooth stroke; tactile, with a bump; and clicky, a tactile switch with an added click. The guide to linear, tactile and clicky switches compares them, and the switch database lists 1,162 switches with their forces and travel.

Reviews of individual boards fill in the feel. RTINGS found that the rubber dome switches of the HyperX Alloy Core RGB "feel unresponsive due to their high pre-travel distance" and wrote that rubber dome switches "can be tiring." It found the switches of the Logitech Wave Keys "somewhat mushy," and notes that typists often describe the rubber domes of the Logitech Signature K650 as "somewhat mushy-feeling compared to mechanical switches." In its list of the best wireless keyboards, RTINGS places the scissor keys of the Keychron B33 and B36 in between: less mushy than the K650's rubber domes and less well-defined than mechanical switches.

Which is quieter, mechanical or membrane?

Membrane keyboards, "in almost all cases," says RTINGS, which describes their sound as "soft, muted." Its review of the Wave Keys rates the typing noise "extremely quiet." Mechanical boards are "typically louder," but the range is wide: RTINGS ranks linear switches among the quietest, tactile slightly louder and clicky loudest, and says silent mechanical switches with dampening "can produce a typing experience nearly as quiet as that of a membrane keyboard." The quiet switches guide lists the silent mechanical options.

Which lasts longer, and which can you repair?

Mechanical boards, by their ratings and by design. RTINGS reports that mechanical switches are "often rated for 50 to 100 million keystrokes" and most membrane keyboards "for around 5 to 10 million." It adds that "these ratings aren't guarantees," and that a keyboard's real life also depends on the case, PCB, stabilizers and solder joints.

Makers publish ratings in the same range. Cherry rates its MX2A Red at 100 million keystrokes, and Realforce's R3S pages rate the 45 g Topre switch at 100 million as well. Among membrane boards, Lenovo rates the keys of its Legion K310 RGB for 20 million presses, above RTINGS's typical range. Of the 1,162 switches in the database, 491 state a rated life: the median is 70 million, 469 are rated at 50 million or more, and the range runs from 10 million on Kailh's Choc Jade and Choc Navy to 500 million on the Wuque Studio WS Pearl.

RTINGS says most mechanical boards let you replace keycaps and stabilizers, and switches with some soldering, while membrane boards are "difficult to repair and typically must be replaced when a key fails." Hot-swap sockets remove the soldering: 593 of the 989 boards in the petitdoigt keyboard database have them in some form, and none of the 31 boards that name scissor, rubber dome or membrane keys does. The hot-swappable keyboard guide explains the sockets. Membrane boards have one durability point in their favour: RTINGS calls their layered construction "semi-sealed," with some protection from minor spills and debris, though not waterproof.

Are membrane keyboards cheaper?

Yes, at the low end. RTINGS puts "the simplest wired membrane keyboard models" regularly under $30 and wireless or RGB membrane models typically under $100, against budget mechanical boards under $100 and custom mechanical builds of $500 or more. The cheapest board in the database is a membrane one, MSI's FORGE GK100 at $24.99.

The database holds mostly mechanical boards: 31 of its 989 name scissor, rubber dome or membrane keys. The 11 priced rubber dome and hybrid membrane boards have a median list price of $54.99, the 16 priced scissor boards $99, and the 760 priced boards with other switches $129.99. Mechanical prices start close to membrane ones: the ROYAL KLUDGE RK61 lists at $34.99 and the Hall effect MonsGeek FUN60 Pro at $29.99.

Is a mechanical keyboard better for typing or gaming?

For gaming, RTINGS favours mechanical boards; for typing, the choice comes down to feel, noise and height. RTINGS says mechanical keyboards often have better matrix scanning and firmware, with "much lower and more consistent input latency," and that virtually all support N-key rollover, while most membrane boards support 6-key rollover. It still calls a membrane keyboard "more than sufficient" for casual gaming, and says gaming membrane boards such as the SteelSeries Apex 3 and Razer Cynosa V2 may use better scanning, better firmware or "hybrid mechanical domes" to close part of the gap.

For typing, RTINGS calls mechanical boards ideal for "gamers, typists, and users who want a consistent and satisfying feel" and membrane boards adequate for "basic typing, office work, or casual gaming" and noise-sensitive spaces. It also notes that membrane boards typically have lower profiles, "which can be more comfortable for extended typing without a wrist rest," and that taller mechanical boards may need one. The guide to ergonomic keyboards covers keyboard shape. Both types come in the standard layouts, such as the 104-key full size of MSI's FORGE GK100, so finger placement carries over from one to the other; the guide to the best switches for typing compares mechanical switches for that use.

To compare switch sounds before you buy, petitdoigt plays key sounds recorded from 13 real switches, chosen from a dropdown in its settings, among them Cherry MX switches, a buckling spring and Topre's rubber dome over a spring; none is a plain membrane board.

What are the best membrane keyboards?

For a membrane board, compare noise, height, spill resistance and price. In its list of the best wireless keyboards, RTINGS picks the Logitech Signature K650 as its budget choice for its built-in wrist rest, deep-set keys and "very quiet" rubber dome switches, with two AA batteries rated for up to 36 months; it lacks backlighting and multi-device pairing. Its cheap pick is the scissor-switch Keychron B33, or the full-size B36, with 2.4 GHz and Bluetooth and batteries rated for up to 320 hours.

For a quiet membrane keyboard, RTINGS rates the Logitech Wave Keys good for office use and "extremely quiet," with a curved, tented layout, a palm rest and pairing with up to three devices, though its rubber domes feel a bit mushy. Other boards in the database list spill features: Lenovo's Legion K310 has spill drain holes, MSI's VIGOR GK30 a water-repellent design and the Glorious GMBK 75 an IP57 rating. PC Gamer scored the GMBK 75 60/100, listing its heavy-feeling rubber domes against it and noting that customisable mechanical boards are more common at its price.

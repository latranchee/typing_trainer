A wireless mechanical keyboard uses the same switches as a wired one and adds a radio and a battery. The radio is Bluetooth, a 2.4 GHz USB receiver (often called a dongle), or both, and most boards keep a cable mode as well. Those parts decide how quickly a key press reaches the computer, how many devices the board can switch between and how often it needs charging. Of the 989 boards in the petitdoigt keyboard database, 528 list Bluetooth or a 2.4 GHz receiver.

What is a wireless mechanical keyboard?

It is a keyboard with discrete mechanical switches that can send keystrokes without a cable. A board with Bluetooth, a receiver and a cable is sold as tri-mode: Logitech uses the word for its G915 X LIGHTSPEED, which connects over its LIGHTSPEED receiver, over Bluetooth or over the included cable. Tri-mode is the most common wireless setup in the database.

Bluetooth vs 2.4 GHz vs wired: which connection should you use?

The 2.4 GHz receiver is the gaming link and Bluetooth is the convenience link. Bluetooth needs no receiver and pairs with several devices, so one board can serve a laptop and a tablet. The receiver occupies a USB port. In RTINGS's measurements, receivers reached 1,000 to 8,000 Hz, while the Keychron K Pro dropped to 125 Hz over Bluetooth. In its review of the NuPhy WH80, RTINGS notes that Bluetooth switches between up to three paired devices, "though the receiver connection is best for gaming."

Does a wireless keyboard add latency? Polling rate claims explained

Over a 2.4 GHz receiver, the boards RTINGS tested here measured low latency; over Bluetooth, several fell well behind. Polling rate is "how often the keyboard reports data to the device it's connected to," in Corsair's words, counted in Hz. At 125 Hz the board reports every 8 ms, at 1,000 Hz every millisecond and at 8,000 Hz every 0.125 ms. Corsair adds that "for regular computer use, high polling rates are not necessary."

RTINGS measures latency with a solenoid that presses the key and a USB analyzer that catches the signal on its way to the computer. It averages 200 presses and sets each board to its fastest settings, including the highest polling rate. Its results on wireless boards show how far an advertised rate can sit from what arrives:

  • The Keychron K2 Pro and its siblings have Bluetooth and no receiver. Wirelessly, the polling rate drops to 125 Hz, with "significantly higher latency" than over the cable.
  • The ROYAL KLUDGE RK61 polls at 1,000 Hz on paper, and RTINGS found that "a low effective update rate of 250Hz bottlenecks" it.
  • The Logitech G915 X runs at 1,000 Hz, a rate RTINGS says "isn't bottlenecked by other latency elements."
  • Keychron's Q Ultra 8K boards reach an effective 8,000 Hz wired or over the receiver. RTINGS still found that the increase to 8,000 Hz "hasn't yielded any tangible improvement in latency."
  • On the K2 Ultra 8K, RTINGS credits the 8,000 Hz rate with keeping latency consistent.

The database records each board's highest wired polling rate. Among the 528 wireless boards, 65 list 8,000 Hz, 191 list 1,000 Hz, one lists 3,000 Hz and 271 state none. The wireless rate can be lower, so look for the figure the maker gives for each connection.

How long does a wireless mechanical keyboard battery last?

From under two days to several months, depending on the board and on the backlight in the makers' own figures. Logitech gives three numbers for one G915 X battery, and Keychron quotes its long figure with the backlight off.

Can a wireless keyboard connect to several devices?

Many can over Bluetooth, with keys or shortcuts that switch between paired devices. RTINGS lists up to three Bluetooth devices on Keychron's K2 Ultra 8K, Q Ultra 8K and K Pro boards and on the RK61. HHKB's HYBRID Type-S pairs with up to 4 and switches with keyboard shortcuts. The ZMK firmware supports five Bluetooth profiles by default, one per paired host. The Logitech G915 X is the exception among the boards here, with no multi-device pairing at all, so check the count on the maker's page if you move between computers.

Which firmware do wireless boards run: QMK, VIA or ZMK?

Firmware decides whether you can remap keys and add layers, and on wireless boards it also affects battery life. QMK documents limited Bluetooth support: "Currently Bluetooth support is limited to AVR based chips," with two supported modules, and those chipsets do not support N-key rollover. VIA, a configuration app for QMK boards, says it is "compatible with 1400+ keyboards and easily added to other QMK keyboards." Keychron lists both on its wireless Pro and Max boards. In the database, Keychron accounts for 101 of the 157 wireless boards that list QMK and 105 of the 181 that list VIA.

ZMK's documentation calls it "an open source (MIT) keyboard firmware built on the Zephyr™ Project Real Time Operating System," designed for "power-efficiency, flexibility, and broad hardware support." Its feature list includes low-latency Bluetooth, wireless split keyboards, multi-device Bluetooth and battery level reporting. In the database, 60 wireless boards list ZMK: half are prebuilt, including 23 Keychrons, 21 of them from the Ultra 8K lines, and 29 are kits, most of them split designs from makers such as splitkb and beekeeb. RTINGS reports that Keychron's battery estimate for the Q Ultra 8K rose from 180 to 660 hours "thanks to the more power-efficient ZMK firmware." The guide to split keyboards covers the kits.

What should you check before buying a wireless mechanical keyboard?

Read the spec sheet for each connection separately, since one board can behave like three different keyboards. Check these points on the maker's page or in a measured review:

  • Connections. A receiver for gaming, Bluetooth for tablets and phones, a cable mode for charging days.
  • Polling rate per connection. A headline 8,000 Hz may apply only to the cable or the receiver.
  • Device count. The Keychron and Logitech boards here pair with three devices, the HHKB HYBRID with four and the G915 X with one.
  • Battery with and without backlight. The G915 X lasts more than twenty times longer with RGB off, and some boards run on AA or AAA cells.
  • Firmware and remapping. QMK, VIA or ZMK let you remap keys; proprietary firmware relies on the maker's app. Of the 528 wireless boards, 159 list proprietary firmware and 140 state none.
  • Hot-swap sockets. 379 wireless boards in the database take replacement switches without soldering, 113 do not and 36 do not say. The hot-swappable keyboard guide explains socket types.
  • Operating systems. Brands state support unevenly: 271 wireless boards list Windows, 246 macOS and 54 iOS.

Switch choice works the same as on a wired board; the keyboard switches guide compares the families. To hear the difference before buying, petitdoigt plays key sounds recorded from 13 real switches, chosen from a dropdown in its settings, while you type its word drills. To narrow the 528 boards to a short list, describe what you want to the keyboard finder in plain words, such as a tri-mode TKL with hot-swap sockets under $150.

Which wireless mechanical keyboards do reviewers recommend?

RTINGS's list of the best wireless keyboards, updated in November 2026, names three mechanical boards. The Keychron Q6 Ultra 8K is its overall pick, for its build, 2.4 GHz and Bluetooth connections, hot-swappable PCB and "exceptionally low latency suitable for competitive play"; RTINGS warns that the Q series is heavy and tall. The Keychron K2 Ultra 8K is its mid-range pick, with 8,000 Hz over the cable and the receiver and three Bluetooth devices. For gaming it names the NuPhy WH80, which uses magnetic switches with adjustable actuation and Rapid Trigger and polls at 8,000 Hz over its receiver; the Hall effect switches guide explains how those work, and 72 wireless boards in the database have adjustable actuation.

The same list's budget and cheap picks use other switch types: the Logitech Signature K650 has rubber domes, and the Keychron B33 and B36 have scissor switches, which RTINGS describes as less mushy than rubber domes and less well-defined than mechanical switches.

The database holds 80 full-size wireless boards, at least 12 of them with scissor or rubber dome keys, and 30 wireless 60% boards, covered in the guide to 60% keyboards. Low-profile boards make up 120 of the wireless boards; their switches are compared in low profile switches.

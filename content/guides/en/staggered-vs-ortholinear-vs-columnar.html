Staggered, ortholinear and columnar are three ways to arrange the same keys on a board. A staggered keyboard offsets each row sideways. An ortholinear keyboard lines every key up in a grid. A column staggered keyboard, also called a columnar keyboard, keeps the grid's vertical columns and moves each column up or down to match its finger. The shape decides which keys each finger reaches for. The guide to which finger presses which key assumes the staggered board, and the finger table below shows what moves when the board changes. Offsets are given in key units: one unit (1u) is the width of a standard letter key.

What is the difference between staggered, ortholinear and columnar keyboards?

The difference is which direction the keys are offset. Staggered boards shift rows sideways, ortholinear boards shift nothing, and columnar boards shift columns up and down.

Deskthority describes the classic typewriter pattern as double staggered and a layout with no staggering as a matrix or ortho-linear layout. The word "ortho-linear" was reportedly coined in an evaluation of the TypeMatrix keyboard.

Why are keyboards staggered?

Keyboards are staggered because typewriters were. The stagger accommodated the physical links between each key and the typewriter's internal mechanism. The offsets let the key arms sit with equal spacing, and the heights of the arms' risers limited how far each row could move, which fixed the uneven 1/2, 1/4, 1/2 pattern.

Computers have no key arms, and the stagger stayed, because people who had already trained on it could switch without effort. Wikipedia's article on ergonomic keyboards also says the staggered layout can force uncomfortable, repetitive movements and postures, a claim the article itself flags as possibly original research.

On an ANSI board the pattern puts the home row a quarter key right of the top letter row and the bottom row half a key right of the home row. The guide to ISO and ANSI keyboards covers the edge keys that differ between standards; both share these offsets.

What is an ortholinear keyboard?

An ortholinear keyboard has its keys in a rigid grid, so each row and column lines up with its neighbors. Switch and Click describes "perfectly lined rows and columns" with most keys the same size, the space bar sometimes the exception. Examples are the OLKB Planck, a 12x4 grid with 47 or 48 keys, and the Keychron Q15 Max. The ortholinear keyboard guide lists the boards.

Because the keys line up, a finger reaches the key below its home key by moving straight down. A reviewer of the Planck EZ at The Sweet Setup says the grid shortens the distance fingers travel to keys such as Y and B, which sit diagonally from the home row on a staggered board.

What is a column staggered keyboard?

A column staggered keyboard sets keys in straight columns and moves each column vertically to suit its finger. The xcmkb documentation describes keys aligned vertically within each column "but with a stagger to match the natural lengths of your fingers." ZSA says a finger on its columnar boards reaches above and below the home row without going sideways. Kinesis says straight columns match natural finger motion better than staggered rows. These are makers' claims.

Wikipedia names the trade-off: vertical columns without stagger minimize sideways finger movement between rows, and they increase the distance from the index finger's neutral position to some of its secondary keys. Offsetting each column to its finger's length addresses that second point on boards like the Corne, Moonlander and Glove80. Many columnar boards also add thumb clusters, groups of keys pressed with the thumbs. The columnar keyboard guide goes through them.

Which finger presses which key on each shape?

Most letters stay with the finger that already presses them. The keys that move are the ones that sat between two columns on a staggered board, plus the pinky's jobs on boards with thumb clusters. The staggered bottom row sits half a key to the right of the home row, which leaves B midway between G and H. The chart gives it to the left index. On a grid or a columnar board B sits directly below G, so the same finger keeps the key and the reach straightens. The number row changes most. The finger placement guide lists three ways to finger it on a staggered board. A grid with a number row leaves only the column method, because each number sits above the letter its finger already presses.

Thumb clusters move work off the pinkies. Dygma lists a 16-key thumb cluster on the Defy, and MoErgo says all six thumb keys of the Glove80 are reachable without moving the palm. Moving Enter, Backspace or modifiers to the thumbs is a retraining job of its own, since the thumbs in the finger chart only press the space bar.

Colemak-DH has a variant for staggered boards. Its angle mod shifts Z, X and C one place left on the bottom row to add symmetry on a standard staggered board, and the Mod-DH page recommends the original mapping for all other keyboard types, ergonomic and ortholinear boards included. The Colemak guide explains the layout.

How long does it take to get used to an ortholinear or columnar keyboard?

Sources report anything from a day to several months, and none measures it in a study. The Sweet Setup reviewer was back to normal typing speed in less than a day on the Planck EZ, needed about two weeks for layer shortcuts to feel natural, and now finds staggered keyboards uncomfortable. Switch and Click warns that switching to an ortholinear board takes weeks or months, with slower and more error-prone typing meanwhile. Wikipedia says a keyboard with vertical columns can need an adjustment period while the user retrains finger movements.

The 40% grid boards add a second task on top of the shape: numbers and symbols live on layers, reached with a Lower or Raise key. Boards with more rows keep a number row and skip that step. A touch typist's existing finger map transfers, so the touch typing guide remains the starting point, and the guide to typing without looking covers keeping your eyes on the screen while the new reaches settle.

Whether any of the three shapes is healthier is unsettled. Deskthority notes that some people believe matrix layouts help with RSI symptoms and found no research on it. Wikipedia quotes Cornell's guide saying there is not much evidence that ergonomic keyboards can prevent injuries. Kinesis states ergonomic benefits for its tented, concave layout on its product page and cites no study.

Which shape should you choose?

A grid board keeps the finger map of the letters, and each key lines up with the one above and below it. A columnar board offsets columns to the fingers and, with a thumb cluster, adds thumb jobs to learn. A staggered board needs no change and has by far the most models, keycap sets and layouts to pick from: 911 of the 989 boards in the petitdoigt keyboard database. The keyboard finder filters that database by shape when you describe the board you want, such as "column staggered split keyboard under $300".

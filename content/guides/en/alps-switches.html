Alps switches are the keyboard switches that Alps Electric of Japan made from the 1980s, and the ones you find in vintage boards such as the Apple IIc keyboard. Alps made keyboards for the original Macintosh and the first iMac. It began making keyboard switches in 1966, and its common plate-mount SKCL and SKCM line came around 1983. Most were discontinued by 1996. Collectors still hunt them, and Matias builds an Alps-style switch for new keyboards. For the switches that replaced them, see the overview of keyboard switches.

What are Alps SKCM and SKCL switches?

SKCL and SKCM are the "complicated" Alps switches, also called Alps Bigfoot or Alps CM, the most common Alps type in computer keyboards. Alps introduced them around 1983, replacing the earlier SKCC series, and discontinued most types by 1996. SKCL switches are linear. SKCM switches are tactile or clicky, because they add a leaf that creates the tactile bump or the click.

Inside, a slider carries the keycap stem and returns on a spring. A six-part switchplate holds the contacts, and a sprung actuator leaf presses a flap onto a foil membrane. A linear switch has about 10 parts and a SKCM about 11. The tactile leaf takes the slot of the integrated LED, so a tactile Alps cannot also carry an LED. The switch mounts to a plate, takes Alps keycaps and is rated at 20 million keystrokes.

Deskthority splits the series into three generations by appearance: original colors and long switchplates (1983 to 1989), replacement colors with top branding and short switchplates (1989 to 1993), and slit-less "bamboo" shells (1993 to 1996). Much of this history is speculative, and the years record where instances were observed, so they are not official dates.

Why did Alps switches disappear?

Alps stopped making most SKCL and SKCM types by 1996. Alps' own company history lists its 1966 start in keyboard switches and a move into complete keyboards and mice in the 1990s, but says nothing of ending the switch line. GamersNexus says Alps switches "are no longer officially produced, but clones exist" and that demand is lower than for other mechanical switches.

A Deskthority forum thread titled "Why are alps keyboards not as popular?" gives these opinions:

  • One poster says manufacturers moved to cheaper rubber domes, while Cherry kept its tooling busy through durable switches in industrial keyboards, and became the standard once gaming brands arrived.
  • Another says Alps was a large conglomerate and sold off its switch business when rubber domes became the norm and profits fell.
  • Keycap makers focus on MX-compatible designs, which keeps Alps out of the mainstream.
  • Used Alps switches are expensive, need restoration skills and are prone to dirt.

Dirt is a real problem, especially on the slitted "pine" variants. Deskthority recommends ultrasonic cleaning of the sliders and tops plus compressed air for the bases and switchplates, and says lubrication gives only modest improvement.

What are Matias switches and other Alps clones?

Matias is a Canadian company, founded in 1989, that makes Alps-style switches for new keyboards. Its Click page says Matias feared the original Alps switches would stop being produced, which would have ended its Tactile Pro keyboard. Its factory suggested Cherry switches, but Matias felt they sounded and felt cheap, so it spent about two years re-creating the Alps design, which it calls a faithful re-creation with the spring ringing removed and better materials.

Matias says the Click lasts five times the 10 million cycles of traditional Alps switches. It sells a box of 200 Click switches for $50 (part KS101-200) and offers 4,800-piece cartons to manufacturers, each with a one-year limited warranty.

Matias keyboards that use them include the Tactile Pro (Click, $149.95 on 2026-11-23), the Programmable Ergo Pro (Quiet Click, two halves with tenting) and a 60% board whose plate and PCB take Alps switches, sold as a group buy. The petitdoigt keyboard database lists five current Matias boards with these switches, the Mini Tactile Pro and the Dvorak Pro among them. Other Alps clones exist. A forum poster says clone makers often require orders of 100 or more sets, which puts them out of reach of individual buyers.

Where do you find Alps keycaps and switches today?

Alps switches use a rectangular stem, so Cherry MX keycaps do not fit, and GamersNexus calls replacement keycaps nearly impossible to find. Matias's page for its Click switch suggests two sources: keycaps from Signature Plastics, or keycaps salvaged from vintage Alps keyboards.

For switches, the options are Matias's store or the resellers it names (MechanicalKeyboards.com in the USA and The Keyboard Company in Europe), a keyboard that ships with Matias switches, or a vintage board. If you buy a vintage board, remember the clone check: two long side tabs make a genuine Alps likely, four small tabs mean a clone.

Stock changes, so confirm on Matias's store before you buy.

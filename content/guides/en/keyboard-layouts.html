A keyboard layout is the map from each key to the character it types. The key to the right of Tab types Q on a US keyboard and A on a French one, with identical hardware underneath. Your operating system holds the layout, so you can change it in a minute, and the letters printed on the keycaps stay as they were.

Shops also use the word for the board's physical shape (ANSI, ISO, JIS) and for sizes such as tenkeyless or 60%. Those get their own section near the end. Most of this guide is about the character map: what each layout puts where and which one suits you.

What are the types of keyboard layouts?

Keyboard layouts fall into two families. National layouts adapt QWERTY to a language: they add its letters (ñ, ü, é), move punctuation to make room and sometimes swap a few letters. Alternative layouts rearrange the English letters themselves so that frequent letters land under the strongest fingers. The table lists the layouts you are most likely to meet.

How do the national keyboard layouts differ?

National layouts differ mostly in punctuation and accented letters, and their letter rows stay close to QWERTY.

US and UK QWERTY

QWERTY comes from the Sholes and Glidden typewriter, which E. Remington and Sons sold from 1874. The popular story that Christopher Latham Sholes arranged the keys to stop typebars from jamming is contested. The layout stayed as a de facto standard on computers, and rival layouts never caught on, partly because QWERTY keyboards and typing courses were everywhere.

The UK layout keeps the letters and moves symbols. @ and " trade places, £ takes the 3 key, # and ~ get an extra key next to Enter, and the backslash moves to the left of Z. The right Alt becomes AltGr. Enter spans two rows, and the board has 105 keys against 104 on a US Windows keyboard.

Canadian French

French-speaking Canadians commonly type on Canadian French, a QWERTY layout. The letters sit where an English typist expects them, é has its own key to the right of the period, and the accents are dead keys: press ^, then E, to get ê. Some Canadians, mostly in Quebec and New Brunswick, use the Canadian Multilingual Standard (CSA) instead. It types English, French and 12 or more other languages, and it is one of the few Windows layouts with œ.

French AZERTY

AZERTY is used in France, Belgium and some African countries. Compared with QWERTY, A and Q swap, Z and W swap, and M moves to the right of L. The number row types symbols and accented letters (&, é, è, ç, à) without Shift, so every digit needs Shift. France's national standards body published a revised AZERTY standard in April 2019, and Belgium keeps its own variant. The key-by-key differences are in AZERTY vs QWERTY.

German and Swiss QWERTZ

QWERTZ is the standard in Germany, Austria and Switzerland and common across Central Europe. Y and Z swap because Z is much more common than Y in German, and T and Z often follow each other: on a typewriter, putting them on separate hands reduced jams. The umlauts ä, ö and ü have their own keys, and so does ß on German and Austrian boards. Braces, square brackets, the backslash, @ and the pipe all need AltGr, which matters if you write code. The Swiss German layout drops ß, which Swiss German does not use, and puts é, à and è on Shift so French can be typed too. More in QWERTZ vs QWERTY.

Spanish and Latin American

Both Spanish layouts keep the QWERTY letters and put ñ to the right of L. The Spain layout adds a Ç key, places the acute accent next to ñ and types @ with AltGr+2. The Latin American layout, used in Mexico and Central and South America, has no Ç key and no € sign, puts the acute accent next to P and types @ with AltGr+Q. If @ comes out in the wrong place, the computer is probably set to the other Spanish layout.

What are the alternative keyboard layouts?

Alternative layouts move English letters so that frequent ones sit on the home row and the hands reach less.

Dvorak

August Dvorak and William Dealey filed their layout in 1932 and received US patent 2,040,248 in May 1936. The left hand's home row holds the vowels A O E U I, and the right holds the consonants D H T N S. ANSI made Dvorak an alternative standard in the early 1980s, and Windows, macOS and Linux all include it. Of the shortcut keys Z, X, C and V, only X stays on the left hand. The Dvorak keyboard guide covers its history and the speed debate.

Colemak

Shai Coleman released Colemak on 1 January 2006. It moves 17 keys and leaves most punctuation where QWERTY has it. Z, X, C and V stay put, so undo, cut, copy and paste keep their keys, and only two keys switch hands. In the default version, Caps Lock becomes a second Backspace. macOS and Linux include Colemak, and Windows added it in Windows 11 version 24H2. The Colemak guide explains how to learn it.

Colemak-DH

Colemak puts D and H in the two middle columns, which the index fingers reach by sliding sideways. Colemak-DH, launched by SteveP in October 2014, moves both to the bottom row, where the index finger curls down to them. G goes back to its QWERTY key, B moves up to the top row, and H trades places with M. No key changes finger. The project offers versions for ANSI, ISO and matrix keyboards, whose bottom-left rows differ.

Workman

OJ Bucao published Workman in 2010. He judged sideways stretches harder on the wrists than up-and-down motion, so Workman keeps typing out of the middle columns: by his count, about 6% of keystrokes land there, against 12% on Colemak, 14% on Dvorak and 22% on QWERTY. It differs from QWERTY in 21 characters. Z and X keep their keys while C and V shift one key to the right. Linux includes Workman; Windows and macOS need the install files linked from the Workman site.

Which keyboard layout is best?

The best keyboard layout for most people is the national layout of the language they type, because it puts that language's letters and accents on single keys and every computer around them already uses it. Alternative layouts shorten finger paths, and the studies that compared them found little speed to gain. In 1956 Earle Strong ran a study for the US General Services Administration with ten typists per group and found Dvorak no more efficient than QWERTY, though the study has been criticized as biased toward the QWERTY group. In 1990 the economists Stan Liebowitz and Stephen Margolis reviewed the experiments in the Journal of Law and Economics and concluded that the best-documented ones show little or no advantage for Dvorak. Later experiments found that many layouts, some alphabetical ones included, allow similar speeds once typists are trained.

How do I change my keyboard layout on Windows or Mac?

You change the layout in the system settings, and you can keep several installed and flip between them. The keycaps keep their printed letters, so after switching to Colemak your keyboard still shows QWERTY. Changing the keyboard layout leaves the display language of Windows as it was.

Windows 11

  1. Open Start, then Settings › Time & language › Language & region.
  2. Select the three dots next to your display language and choose Language options.
  3. Next to Installed keyboards, select Add a keyboard and pick the layout. If it is missing, add the language it belongs to first.
  4. Switch layouts by holding the Windows key and pressing Space, or from the language icon in the taskbar.

macOS

  1. Open System Settings and click Keyboard in the sidebar.
  2. Under Text Input, click Edit, then the Add button.
  3. Search for the language, select the input source you want, such as Dvorak, and click Add.
  4. Switch from the Input menu in the menu bar, press Control+Space for the last source you used, or set the Globe or fn key to Change Input Source.

Are ANSI, ISO, TKL and 60% keyboard layouts too?

Those names describe the hardware, and a board of any shape or size can run any of the layouts above. ANSI, ISO and JIS are physical layouts: the shape and number of keys. ANSI is the US standard, with a wide one-row Enter key. ISO, used in the UK and across Europe, has a tall Enter key and a shorter left Shift with an extra key beside it. JIS, the Japanese standard, shortens the space bar and Backspace to fit four more keys. ISO vs ANSI keyboards shows how to tell which one you have.

Sizes such as tenkeyless and 60% say how many keys the board keeps. The letter rows are the same at every size, so a 60% board types QWERTY, AZERTY or Colemak like a full-size one.

Can I try a keyboard layout without installing it?

Yes. petitdoigt, a free typing trainer that runs in the browser, applies the layout itself from the physical position of each key, so you can type Colemak, Dvorak, Workman or AZERTY while your system stays on its usual layout. The on-screen keyboard shows the chosen layout's letters, each colored by the finger that should press it, which helps while your keycaps still say QWERTY. It has 11 layouts (Canadian French, US QWERTY, French AZERTY, German and Swiss QWERTZ, Spanish, Latin American, Dvorak, Colemak, Colemak-DH and Workman) and draws ANSI, ISO or JIS boards to match yours.

For a fair test, type common words on Colemak or Dvorak for ten minutes a day over a week before you decide. Your first sets will be slow. The stats page keeps separate numbers for each layout, per finger and per letter, so you can see which keys still cost you time. A browser can't see which finger pressed a key, so the trainer shows the right finger and measures the keys and their timing.

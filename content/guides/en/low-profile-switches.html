A low profile mechanical keyboard swaps the standard MX switch for a shorter one, so the keys sit lower and the stroke is shorter. Four main families cover nearly every board: Kailh Choc (versions 1 and 2), Gateron low profile, Cherry MX Low Profile and the even flatter Cherry MX Ultra Low Profile. Each has its own footprint and keycap stem, which decides what you can swap in. The keyboard switches overview covers the standard switches these are shortened from.

What is a low profile mechanical switch?

A low profile switch is a mechanical switch with a shorter housing and less travel than a standard one. A standard Cherry MX Red lists 4 mm total travel and 2 mm pre-travel on Cherry's page. Cherry's MX Low Profile 2.0 Red lists 3.2 mm total and 1.2 mm pre-travel, and says it is 35 percent lower in height while keeping the typing feel of the MX Red.

Shorter switches allow a slimmer case and a lower typing angle. The price is a shallower stroke and, on some families, keycaps and sockets of their own. Figures are the makers' own, in the unit each uses: Cherry gives cN, Kailh and Gateron give gf, and 1 gf is 0.98 cN per Deskthority's wiki, so the two are close.

What are Kailh Choc switches?

Kailh Choc switches are the low profile family used on many split and DIY keyboards. The first generation, Choc v1, has its own PG1350 footprint, and Kailh's page for the Choc Brown notes that the switches lack the traditional cross stem, so they need compatible keycaps. The Kailh page lists 1.5 mm pre-travel and 3.0 mm total travel.

Choc v2 changed the stem. Deskthority's wiki describes it as using a low-profile Cherry MX-style mount, so it takes the same keycaps as Cherry MX Low Profile switches, short skirt only. It is only partially backward compatible with Choc v1 footprints: the central pin is larger, and the click bar of the clicky version uses a different hole. Kailh lists 1.3 mm pre-travel and 3.2 mm total travel for the standard v2, and 2.8 mm for the silent ones.

Choc v1 appears in 25 boards in the petitdoigt keyboard database. They include the ZSA Voyager, the MoErgo Glove80 and a long list of Corne and Sweep builds, so most are split or small DIY keyboards. PC Gamer scored the Voyager 78/100, calling it as compact as a split board gets and too compromised to suit every user. Notebookcheck described it as expensive but well built, Choc v1 only, with fragile keycaps and no wireless. The split keyboard guide covers that design.

What are Gateron low profile switches?

Gateron's KS-33 family is the low profile line used in many Keychron and NuPhy slim boards. The petitdoigt switch database lists linear, tactile, clicky and silent variants in versions 2.0 and 3.0, with forces from 35 gf to 65 gf. The 3.0 generation lists 3.4 mm total travel on the red and 3.5 mm on the silver, against 3.2 mm in the 2.0 generation.

Compatibility is narrow. Gateron says the KS-33 3.0 fits only the NuPhy Nos75, Kick75, Air75 V3 and Node75. RTINGS describes the NuPhy Air V3 series as low profile wireless boards with a hot-swappable PCB for Gateron low profile 3.0 switches. The petitdoigt keyboard database lists 45 low profile Keychron boards, many with Gateron switches, such as the K3 Max, and RTINGS rated that line as having amazing build quality and excellent all-round performance, even for competitive gaming.

What are Cherry MX Low Profile and ULP switches?

Cherry MX Low Profile is the older MX-style low profile switch, and the ULP is a far flatter one. Deskthority lists the original MX Low Profile at 3.2 mm total travel with 1.2 mm of pre-travel for the Red, with a speed version at 1.0 mm. Boards using it include the Corsair K70 MK.2 Low Profile, the Filco Majestouch Stingray and the Alienware AW510K. The switch uses the Cherry MX cross-pattern mount, short skirt only, and Deskthority says it is not pin-compatible with Cherry MX.

Cherry's page for the MX Low Profile 2.0 Red lists a 45 cN operating force, 30 cN initial force and 100 cN final force, with over 100 million keystrokes. The first keyboards to use it were Cherry's KC 500 MX LP series in 2025, per the petitdoigt switch database.

The ULP is thinner still. Cherry rates it at 1.8 mm total travel, 0.8 mm pre-travel and over 50 million keystrokes, and designs it for SMD assembly. Deskthority lists its keycap mount as proprietary, so standard MX keycaps are not expected to fit. Boards in the petitdoigt keyboard database include the Corsair K100 Air Wireless and the Cherry KW X ULP series, and Deskthority also names the Mistel AIRONE.

Which keycaps and boards fit?

Match the keycaps to the stem, then the switches to the socket:

  • Choc v1 needs keycaps made for its stem.
  • Choc v2 and Cherry MX Low Profile take MX-style cross stems with a short skirt. Deskthority warns that thick-walled MX keycaps can hit the wider housing of Cherry's switch when fully pressed, and thin-walled ones can hit the plate.
  • Gateron low profile stems need keycaps made for them, and the switch fits only the boards its maker lists.
  • Cherry ULP has a proprietary mount.

The petitdoigt keyboard database holds 159 boards flagged as low profile, 17 of which are scissor or laptop-style keyboards such as Apple's Magic Keyboards. Of the rest, 46 use the Gateron low profile footprint, 25 use Choc v1, 11 use Cherry MX Low Profile and 2 use Cherry ULP, and the remaining 58 use other sockets, Logitech's proprietary design on 11 of them. Hot-swap is rarer than on standard boards: 72 of the 159 are hot-swappable, 18 of them with Choc sockets, against 521 of the 830 other boards. Of the low profile boards, 77 are soldered and 10 do not say. The hot-swappable keyboard guide explains why a socket fixes your choice of switch family. To list the boards that match your needs, describe one to the keyboard finder, for example "low profile wireless keyboard with hot-swap", and it filters the database.

Low profile boards also come in the usual sizes; see keyboard sizes to pick one. Magnetic and optical switches exist in low profile form too, for example Gateron's low profile magnetic Jade and Razer's low profile analog optical switch, covered in the Hall effect and optical guides.

Are low profile keyboards good for typing?

Reviewers find them comfortable and quiet, with caveats on feel. Tom's Hardware gave Logitech's G515 Lightspeed TKL 4/5, noting that the flat keys can hinder fast shooters, and TechRadar gave it 4/5 for comfortable, quiet keystrokes. PC Gamer's review of the Logitech G515 Rapid TKL, a magnetic low profile board, said the switches feel hollow for long typing.

No source here compares typing speed on low profile and standard boards. The trainer offers finger-coded drills in the browser, with key sounds recorded from 13 real switches that you choose from a dropdown in its settings, and makes no claim that any switch changes speed. Several families above have silent versions, which the quiet switches guide covers.

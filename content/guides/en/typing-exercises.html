The ring and little fingers are the usual laggards on a keyboard. They miss keys and drag down your speed, and a stronger neighbor often reaches over to cover for them, which builds a habit you later have to undo. The exercises below train those fingers one at a time, with text you can type right now.

Which typing exercises should you start with?

Start with the exercise that matches your level. Beginners work on the home row and add one row at a time; the guide to touch typing walks through that step by step. Typists who already use all ten fingers get more from drills aimed at their slowest fingers, which is what the rest of this page covers.

Why are the ring and pinky fingers weakest at typing?

The ring finger is the least independent finger of the hand. In a 2000 study, researchers recorded ten people moving one finger at a time, with video and an instrumented glove. The ring finger dragged its neighbors along more than any other finger: it had the lowest independence score in the right hand of nine of the ten subjects (Häger-Ross and Schieber). The authors put part of this down to mechanics, since the fingers are linked and several forearm muscles each pull on more than one finger, and part to how the nervous system controls the hand. Fingers in the dominant hand were no more independent than those in the other hand.

The little finger scored better in that study, third after the thumb and index. Its trouble on a keyboard is workload. On US QWERTY the right pinky owns P, ;, /, ' and the bracket keys, and it also presses Enter, right Shift and Backspace. Several of those keys sit far from its home key, Backspace most of all.

These fingers also carry a large share of ordinary text. Add up the frequencies of the letters they type on QWERTY and you get about 31% of the letters in English:

Self-taught typists often use fewer fingers and let the middle or index finger reach over. The How We Type study at Aalto University motion-captured 30 typists and named three predictors of high performance. One of them was unambiguous mapping: each letter pressed by the same finger every time. A ring finger that types L on Monday and gets replaced by the middle finger on Tuesday never builds that consistency.

Do finger strength exercises help typing?

Grip trainers and stress balls build force, and typing needs very little of it. A common mechanical switch, the Cherry MX Red, actuates at 45 centinewtons, roughly the weight of a 46-gram object resting on the key. Every finger can press that. The weak fingers lack control, the ability to press one key cleanly and on time while the neighbors stay put.

You train control at the keyboard, and pace matters. In the same finger study, people moved one finger more independently at their own pace, around two movements a second, than when they followed a metronome at three a second. The authors thought the slower rate explained more of the gap than the metronome did. Slow drills keep you in the range where the finger moves most on its own, and that clean movement is what you speed up later.

What are the best typing exercises for weak fingers?

The typing exercises that work best train one finger at a time, in an order where each step builds on the last: isolated taps first, then words full of that finger's letters, then the awkward letter pairs that trip it up in real text. Go slowly enough to make almost no errors.

1. Isolation taps

An isolation drill moves one finger between its home key and its reach keys while the other fingers of that hand stay on their home keys. With the neighbors pinned in place, only the weak finger can reach the key.

  1. Put your fingers on the home row: A S D F for the left hand, J K L ; for the right.
  2. Pick one finger. Rest the other three fingers of that hand on their home keys and keep them there.
  3. Tap home, reach, home, reach, at a slow and even beat. Say the letters out loud if it helps you keep time.
  4. Do 20 to 30 taps, then move to the next finger.

The line of names trains Shift. Hold Shift with the pinky of the opposite hand: P, O and L take the left Shift, while S, Q, Z, W and X take the right one. Typists who only ever use one Shift key end up holding Shift and typing the letter with the same hand for about half the alphabet, which pulls that hand off the home row.

3. Drills for the awkward letter pairs

Weak fingers often stumble on transitions between two keys. Some pairs need the same finger twice in a row, so it has to travel before the second press. Others are quick rolls between the ring and pinky of one hand, where the ring finger's neighbors tend to move with it. Type each pair on its own five times, then the words.

How long should you practice typing each day?

Short daily sessions teach more per hour of practice than long ones. In a 1978 study for the British Post Office, Baddeley and Longman trained four groups of postmen to type on different schedules. The group that practiced one hour a day learned the whole keyboard in about 35 hours of practice on average. The group that did two 2-hour sessions a day needed about 50 hours, and after 60 hours of training it typed the slowest and left the most errors uncorrected.

That study tested nothing shorter than an hour, so it backs spreading practice across days without naming an ideal session length. For weak-finger work, 15 minutes a day covers all three exercises and ends before fatigue makes your taps sloppy. Fit it in before your usual practice or a typing test, while your hands are fresh.

What does a weekly typing practice routine look like?

A good weekly routine gives each weak finger two focused days and closes every session with a couple of minutes of ordinary typing, to check that the drills carry over. Each 15-minute session follows the same order: about 3 minutes of isolation taps, 5 of practice lines, 5 of letter pairs and 2 of free typing.

Which typing habits keep weak fingers weak?

Most of the habits that hold weak fingers back let another finger, or your eyes, do the weak finger's job:

  • Letting a neighbor cover. The ring finger hitting P, or the middle finger taking L, feels faster today and leaves the weak finger untrained. The keyboard finger chart shows which finger owns every key.
  • Pushing speed too early. Every error you type at speed is a repetition of the wrong movement. The guide to typing accuracy explains why accuracy comes first and how to raise it.
  • Looking down at the keys. Your eyes pull the hand off the home row and hide which finger moved. See how to type without looking at the keyboard.
  • The WASD habit. Gamers who rest on WASD put the ring finger on A, which belongs to the pinky in touch typing. Carried over to typing, that habit leaves the left pinky with no key of its own.
  • Practicing only what feels good. Speed tests reward the strong fingers. Spend your drill minutes on the letters and pairs where your numbers are worst.

If a finger aches or tingles during these drills, stop for the day, and see a doctor if the pain keeps coming back.

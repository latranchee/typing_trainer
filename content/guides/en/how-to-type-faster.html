Most of the gap between slow and fast typists lies in the time between keys. In a 2018 study of 168,000 online volunteers by researchers at Aalto University and the University of Cambridge, the fastest tenth held each key down for 104 milliseconds on average and the slowest tenth for 129. The time from one keystroke to the next was four times longer for the slow group: 481 milliseconds against 122. You shrink that time by knowing which finger moves next without thinking and by wasting fewer keystrokes on errors. Fast typists also start the next finger on its way before the current key comes back up.

How to type faster in six steps

Build accuracy and fixed finger habits first, then train your weak spots in short daily sessions. The sections below explain each step and the research behind it.

  1. Give every key one finger and use that finger every time.
  2. Keep your eyes on the screen, reading a word or two ahead of your fingers.
  3. Slow down until errors are rare, and fix each one the moment it happens.
  4. Find your slowest letter pairs and weakest fingers, then drill them in short sets.
  5. Push past your comfortable speed for a few minutes a day and work on whatever breaks.
  6. Test yourself once a week on the same kind of text to see the trend.

What do fast typists do differently?

Fast typists make fewer mistakes and often press the next key before letting go of the previous one. The 2018 study compared the fastest 10 percent of participants (above about 78 WPM) with the slowest 10 percent (below about 26 WPM):

Rollover is the habit of pressing the next key while the last one is still down. Across all participants it tracked speed closely (a correlation of 0.73), and when it happened the two keys overlapped by about 30 milliseconds on average. The researchers saw it in fast typists whether or not they had learned touch typing. People who had taken a typing course were only about 5 WPM faster on average than those who hadn't.

A smaller 2016 study from Aalto tracked the hands and eyes of 30 everyday typists with motion capture and eye-tracking glasses. The self-taught typists averaged 58.9 WPM and those trained in touch typing 57.8 WPM. Three habits predicted speed: pressing each letter with the same finger every time, moving toward the next key during the current keystroke, and keeping the hands still over the keyboard. The self-taught typists, however, spent 41 percent of their typing time looking at the keyboard, about twice the touch typists' 20 percent.

Which finger should press each key?

Give every key one finger and use that finger every time. The standard assignment, shown below, gives each finger a slanted column of keys: the index fingers take two columns each, the little fingers take the outer edge, and the thumbs share the space bar. Your fingers rest on the home row (A S D F and J K L ; on QWERTY), and the small bumps on F and J let you find it by touch. The keyboard finger chart covers the edge cases, such as which Shift to use.

If you already type with six or seven fingers, you may not need to start over. Check whether each letter always goes to the same finger and whether you can type a sentence with your eyes on the screen. If either check fails, switch to the standard assignment, which fixes a finger for every key from the first day. A home-made system that sends E to the middle finger one time and the index the next forces a choice on every keystroke, and the 2016 researchers suggest that a fixed finger for each key removes that choice along with much of the need to look down. Should you slow down to type faster?

Yes. Slow down until mistakes are rare, because each error costs far more time than one wrong keystroke. You notice it, stop, press Backspace once or several times, find your place and type the word again. In the 2018 data, people who made and corrected fewer errors were faster, and the authors point out that reducing errors by a small margin can bring a large gain in speed. The slowest typists most often hit a wrong letter, an error the researchers suspect comes from inconsistent typing and a weak sense of where the fingers are.

If you fix more than a word or two per line, you are typing faster than your fingers know the keys. Drop your pace until whole lines come out clean and let speed return on its own. Two habits help:

  • Correct each error the moment it happens. Practicing in a mode that won't move on until you erase the typo makes every mistake cost something, and you soon start to avoid them.
  • Watch accuracy next to speed. Net speed, which subtracts errors, is the number that matters in real work; how words per minute is calculated explains gross and net WPM.

How do you find the letter pairs that slow you down?

Time each transition, the move from one key to the next, and drill the slowest ones. Speed is built from familiar sequences: in the 2016 study, typing random strings of letters cut people's speed by about half compared with real sentences. The 2018 study found that the speed of letter pairs typed with two different fingers or two hands predicted overall speed far better than double letters like ll or ee, which slow and fast typists alike press quickly.

On QWERTY, some common pairs make one finger press both keys, so there is no chance to overlap them. Examples: ed and ce (left middle finger), un and my (right index), br and gr (left index), lo and ol (right ring), ki (right middle). Everyday words hide them: used, under, great.

Your own slow pairs depend on your hands and habits, so measure them. Without a tool, type a paragraph, mark the words where you hesitate, and turn the troublesome pair into a practice line: ed used need ended edited.

Weak fingers respond to the same treatment. If your ring and little fingers lag, train them alone with short sets that tap a key and return to the home row; the typing exercises guide has a weekly routine for them.

How long should you practice typing each day?

Plan on 10 to 15 focused minutes a day, spent above your comfortable speed and on your weak spots. Early studies of skilled typists, reviewed by Keith and Ericsson in 2007, suggest pushing your speed beyond its comfortable level for short, concentrated periods of 10 to 15 minutes a day, then working on the difficulties those periods expose, such as typing errors and slow transitions. Volume alone does less. In Keith and Ericsson's own study of 60 university students with years of typing experience, how much they typed in a typical week showed no clear link with their speed, though their total typing since they first used a keyboard did. The best performers had taken a typing class and also tried to type fast in their everyday typing.

A 15-minute session, split into five blocks:

  1. Warm-up, 2 minutes. Common words at a relaxed pace, eyes on the screen.
  2. Weak pairs, 5 minutes. The transitions your measurements flag, in short sets. End a set early when errors pile up.
  3. Weak fingers, 3 minutes. Isolation taps for your slowest finger, back to the home row each time.
  4. Speed push, 3 minutes. Sentences at a fixed tempo a few WPM above your comfortable speed. Errors will rise here, and the next block cleans them up.
  5. Clean finish, 2 minutes. Sentences at your normal pace with the goal of zero errors.

In petitdoigt those blocks map to the words, pairs, anchors and highway drills. The highway drill can hold a fixed tempo and refuses keys pressed early, which keeps the speed push honest. Test yourself once a week on the same kind of text, so each result compares like with like.

How do you increase typing speed when you're stuck?

Find the one thing holding your speed down and practice it deliberately. Everyday typing settles at a speed that is good enough for the work and rarely pushes you past it. Keith and Ericsson describe deliberate practice as working against that automaticity on purpose. The 2016 Aalto researchers add that for touch typists, reaching and holding more than 70 WPM takes some form of deliberate practice. When your number stops moving, check these in order:

  • Errors creeping back. If Backspace shows up in most lines again, spend a week at a slower pace with accuracy as the only goal.
  • Eyes drifting down. Notice whether you glance at the keys for numbers, symbols or capital letters, and practice text that contains them.
  • A few slow transitions. At 70 WPM your average keystroke takes about 170 milliseconds. A handful of common pairs that take twice as long can hold back a speed your other keys already support. Look at your slowest pairs and drill them.
  • No rollover. Practice common pairs such as th, in and an as single smooth movements, pressing the second key before the first comes up.
  • Reading too close. Skilled typists read ahead of the key they are pressing. In the experiments Keith and Ericsson review, blocking that preview cut skilled typists' advantage almost to the level of novices. Keep your eyes a word or two ahead of your fingers.

How do you type 100 WPM?

Typing 100 WPM means one keystroke every 120 milliseconds on average with almost no corrections, which takes a fixed finger for every key, eyes reading ahead and plenty of rollover. A "word" in typing tests is any five characters, spaces included, so 100 WPM equals 500 characters a minute, a little over eight a second.

That pace sits above the average of the fastest tenth in the 2018 data, who typed about 90 WPM, and the fastest participants reached 120 WPM or more. Errors have to be rare enough that Backspace almost disappears. Rollover carried about half of the fast group's keystrokes, so practice it once your accuracy holds. After that, the gain sits in your slowest transitions: keep measuring them as you climb. For how 100 WPM compares with typists in general, see average typing speed by level.

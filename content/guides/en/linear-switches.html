Linear switches are the mechanical switches with a smooth stroke: no bump you feel at the actuation point and no click. They make up one of the three feels in the guide to keyboard switches, next to tactile and clicky ones. Common examples below carry the specs their makers publish.

What makes a switch linear?

A linear switch has a stem without a bump mechanism, so the force rises steadily with the spring until the stem hits the bottom. Deskthority's Cherry MX page describes linear as smooth travel with no tactile bump or click, and lists Black, Red and Speed Silver as the linear Cherry switches. A tactile switch adds a bump at the actuation point and a clicky switch adds a click; the linear stem does neither.

You see the difference on a spec sheet: linear switches list an actuation force and a bottom-out force and no bump force. Cherry's MX2A Red gives 45 cN to actuate and 100 cN at the bottom, which means the spring does the work across a 4 mm stroke. The same MX2A family's Brown, a tactile switch, adds a 55 cN bump peak at the same 45 cN actuation force.

An ortholinear keyboard has nothing to do with linear switches: it lays its keys in a straight grid and takes any switch type. The guide to staggered vs ortholinear vs columnar covers the layouts.

What are common linear switches and their specs?

Every large maker sells a light, a medium and a heavy linear. The petitdoigt switch database holds 682 linear switches from 47 makers, 135 of them from Gateron, plus 90 silent linears. Gateron's Yellow name covers more than one switch. The G Pro 3.0 Yellow and the KS-3X1 Milky Yellow Pro both list 50 gf, while the Ink V2 Yellow lists 60 gf and a 1.2 mm pre-travel. Check the line name, the product page and the force before buying.

Makers and labs do not always agree. Gateron lists the Oil King at 55 gf actuation and about 80 gf at the bottom; RTINGS measured 48 gf actuation, 61 gf bottom-out and 2.1 mm pre-travel in its lab test. ThereminGoat calls it a solidly executed heavy linear with mixed housing materials and a long spring, and scores it 78/100.

What are speed and silent linear switches?

Speed linears shorten the distance to actuation, and silent linears damp the sound. Both are linear switches with one change.

  • Speed. Cherry's MX2A Speed Silver actuates after 1.2 mm of a 3.4 mm stroke at 45 cN, against 2 mm of 4 mm for the Red. Gateron's G Pro Silver lists 1.2 mm and 3.4 mm as well, and Kailh and Akko sell silver and speed variants too. A shorter pre-travel reaches the actuation point sooner; it does not change the force.
  • Silent. Cherry's MX2A Silent Red keeps the Red's 45 cN and 100 cN and actuates at 1.9 mm of 3.7 mm; Cherry's MX2A Northern Light lists 45 cN, 1.6 mm and 3.7 mm. Gateron's Oil King Pink Linear Silent lists 45 gf and 3.8 mm total. Matias's Quiet Linear is an Alps-style switch with a red stem and an internal leaf spring that evens out the force across the stroke; it lists 35 gf at both actuation and the bottom.
  • Magnetic. Hall effect switches are linear too: Wooting's Lekker V2 L45 starts the press at 30 gf and ends at 45 gf, with the actuation point set in software. See Hall effect switches.

The guide to quiet switches collects the silent options of every feel.

How heavy should a linear switch be?

Force class is the first choice. Published figures fall into light, medium and heavy groups:

  • Light, 20 to 40 force units. Kailh's Choc Pro Pink (20 gf, low profile), Cherry's MX Blossom (35 cN), Akko's Dracula (35 gf) and Wooting's Lekker V2 L45 (30 gf to start, 45 gf at the bottom).
  • Medium, 45 to 55. Cherry's MX2A Red (45 cN), Gateron's G Pro Yellow (50 gf), Wooting's Lekker Tikken Medium (42 gf to 54 gf) and Gateron's Oil King (55 gf).
  • Heavy, 60 and up. Cherry's MX2A Black (60 cN) and Gateron's Ink V2 Black (60 gf). Kailh's Box Dark Yellow lists 70 gf and its Box Ancient Grey 95 gf.

The bottom-out force matters too: Gateron's G Pro 3.0 Red bottoms out at 50 gf and Cherry's MX2A Red at 100 cN, so two switches at the same 45 on paper end their stroke with different resistance. Factory lubrication, housing materials and spring length vary by line and sit on the product page; the guide to lubing switches covers what lube changes.

Are linear switches good for typing or gaming?

No source has shown linear switches to be faster or more accurate than tactile ones for either. A linear switch gives every key the same smooth press, and what changes the experience is force: a 35 gf spring and a 60 cN spring feel different over a long session, regardless of the bump question.

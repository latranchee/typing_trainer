Keyboard sizes are named by how much of the full-size layout a board keeps. A full-size board has the alphabet block, the function row, the navigation keys, the arrows and a number pad. Smaller sizes remove those groups from the outside in, and the percentage names say roughly how much is left. The letters sit in the same place on every size, so a touch typist's home row never moves; the sizes differ in which other keys sit within reach.

The wiki's 40% row also loses the number row and some punctuation from the alphanumeric block. Its 65% and 75% rows treat the navigation cluster as present or missing as a whole, while real boards keep a few of its keys: a 65% board keeps Delete, Page Up and Page Down, says Corsair. Corsair also lists 99% and 96% sizes, where the navigation cluster is squeezed beside the arrows and a couple of keys are dropped, and states that exact key counts and positions vary by model, most of all on 96%, 75% and 65% boards.

ISO boards carry one more key than their ANSI twins and have a taller Enter, so a full-size ISO board counts 105 and an ISO TKL counts 88. The guide to ISO vs ANSI keyboards covers the shape of Enter and Shift.

What does each size drop?

Each step removes a group of keys. Corsair describes them this way:

  • Full size drops nothing. Corsair says it suits data entry, spreadsheets and keybind-heavy games, and is a poor fit for low-sensitivity first-person players who need mouse space.
  • 96% and 99% keep the number pad and cut the gap between the blocks, which Corsair says helps in a cramped setup. A programmer who leans on the navigation cluster will need to relearn some key positions.
  • TKL removes only the number pad. Corsair names it for people who want to lose the pad and keep the function row and arrows.
  • 75% keeps the function row and arrows in a narrower body. Compared with a TKL, keys such as Print Screen, Scroll Lock, Pause, Insert and End are often missing, and the right-hand keys vary by brand.
  • 65% keeps the arrows, Delete, Page Up and Page Down and drops the function row. Some models add extra keys, such as the G1 to G4 on Corsair's K65 PRO MINI.
  • 60% keeps the alphanumeric keys and modifiers only. Corsair calls it the best for competitive first-person shooters and recommends a separate number pad when you do heavy data work.

The 40% size and the separate number pad sit at the two ends. At 40% the number row is gone. A number pad on its own, such as the Keychron Q0 Max or the Realforce RT1 with 23 keys, is the number-pad block sold as its own board, usually used beside a 60% or 65% board. The finger chart in the guide to finger placement shows the block that every size keeps.

The 75% size is the largest group in the database at 207 boards, followed by full size at 190 and TKL at 161. The 40% row holds eight boards, among them the Planck EZ and the Equals 48, which place their keys in a straight grid. The guides to ortholinear keyboards and split keyboards explain why those boards fall outside the usual percentages.

Size also lines up with other features in the database. Of the 207 boards at 75%, 158 are hot swappable, against 72 of 190 full-size boards, which the guide to hot swappable keyboards explains. Low-profile boards, built around the shorter switches described in low-profile switches, show up at every size.

Which keyboard size should you choose?

The keys you use daily decide it. Count how often you reach for a number pad, the function row and the arrows, and choose the smallest size that keeps those. Sources name these factors:

  • Number pad. Spreadsheets and data entry favor full size or 96%, according to Corsair. Corsair rates the TKL strong for first-person shooters and weaker for heavy spreadsheet work.
  • Mouse space. Removing the pad frees room on the right of the desk. Corsair mentions first-person players who need mouse space.
  • Arrows and function row. Both stay on a 75%. The 65% keeps the arrows only, and the 60% keeps neither, so those keys are missing from the board.
  • Desk width. Corsair recommends the 60% for small desks.

A spec sheet cannot show which missing keys you will reach for. Typing something you write every day on a layout without them shows quickly which ones you miss.

Lubing switches means applying a thin layer of grease or oil to the parts of a mechanical switch that rub together. Hobbyists do it to smooth out a scratchy keypress and, some say, to soften the sound. Many switches now arrive lubed, so the question is often whether to add more. The switches themselves are described in the guide to keyboard switches.

What does lubing switches do?

Lube cuts friction between the stem and the housing, and it can calm spring noise. Lube gives a smoother press, a quieter board, and less reverberation (the "ping") when it sits on the coiled spring. Thinner lubes go on sliders and rails, and thicker ones on the bars of stabilizers, the long stems under wide keys.

A Cherry MX-style switch has four places where parts slide. Lube goes on the rails of the bottom housing and the stem areas that touch them. On linear switches it also goes on the stem legs and the leaf contact area, and optionally on the bend of the leaf to reduce ping. Springs get brushed with grease or bag-lubed with oil. Kinetic Labs adds the top housing, which it says to skip unless a switch is scratchy and not yet broken in.

Lube does nothing for the force numbers on a switch's spec sheet, which describe the spring and the stem shape. The guide to switch specs explains those figures.

Kinetic Labs adds two practical points about 205g0. Tactile switches can take a very light layer, and the leaf and the front legs of the stem should stay clean. The shop also says to check that a listing says "Grade 0", since other Krytox greases carry other grades, and that unbranded "GPL 205g0" listings may be different products. MakeUseOf names Krytox 205g0 or Super Lube Synthetic Grease as the main lube in its walk-through.

One hard limit applies: no lube on electrical contacts. Spray lubing is not recommended because solvents can dissolve parts and melt rubber dampers.

Do factory-lubed switches need more lube?

A factory-lubed switch is ready to use, and whether it gains from more lube is a matter of taste. Makers apply lube in the plant, and many name it on the product page. Gateron states it for the Jupiter and Milky Pro lines, ASUS states it for its ROG NX switches, and Akko sells its CS Piano switch pre-lubed with Krytox GPL 105 and GPL 205 G0.

In the petitdoigt switch database, 569 of the 1,162 switches are recorded as factory-lubed, 85 as not lubed and 508 have no stated lubing. By maker, Gateron has 171 factory-lubed switches in the database, HMX 41, TTC 33, Akko 29, Bsun 28 and Haimu 28, which is every Haimu switch the database lists. The Deskthority wiki adds that Cherry MX White may be factory-lubed, possibly to reduce click sound, and that some Alps SKCM switches have been found with factory lube.

Maker pages say that a switch is lubed but rarely with what or how evenly.

What are switch films, and are they part of lubing?

Films belong to the same tuning habit. A film is a thin sheet that sits between the top and bottom housings of a switch. Kinetic Labs says films seal the gap between the housings and stop the top housing from wobbling. They do not affect stem wobble, since they touch only the housings.

Films can change the sound by damping noise inside the switch or reducing vibration passed through the housing, and that the result for a given switch and film cannot be predicted. Films are mostly needed on older switches with looser housings, because modern switches have better tolerances. A switch with very tight tolerances may have no room for a film: forcing one in can damage the housing or the plate or stop the switch from closing. Kinetic Labs advises testing one or two switches with films in your keyboard before modifying a whole batch.

How do you lube switches, step by step?

Pull the switches, open them, brush a thin layer on the sliding parts, and close them again. The sequence follows the Kinetic Labs walk-through for 205g0 and assumes a hot-swappable board; the guide to hot swappable keyboards explains how the sockets work.

  1. Gather a switch opener, a size 0 or 00 flat brush (Kinetic Labs also names a pointed 0/2/0), a stem holder and tweezers. Clean the switches first, since the wiki says dirt reduces the lube's effect.
  2. Open each switch and keep its parts together in a container.
  3. Load both sides of the brush, coat each rail of the bottom housing equally, paint the leaf and brush around the center hole.
  4. Coat the spring with a thin layer and leave no blobs, or bag-lube springs: MakeUseOf uses Krytox 105g0 in a zip bag, and Kinetic Labs describes a pea-sized dollop shaken for a couple of minutes.
  5. Hold the stem and apply equal amounts to each slider, then coat the front, back and legs. The pole is optional.
  6. Skip the top housing unless the switch is scratchy.
  7. Reassemble. Kinetic Labs describes a well-lubed switch as having an even, shiny coat with no white blobs.

Less is better, according to both Kinetic Labs and MakeUseOf. Kinetic Labs says it is easier to add lube than to remove it, and that its 5 ml bottle can cover more than 500 switches with a thin layer. Too much lube on a spring can interfere with the switch, and the wiki tells you to wipe the side of the slider on tactile and clicky switches to preserve their feel.

MakeUseOf describes an easier route that skips disassembly: push the stem down, brush lube into the gap and tap the switch to spread it. The author calls it faster, with some remaining spring ping.

What do sources say about the results?

Sources report smoother keys, and they split on sound. Kinetic Labs says 205g0 "tends to give your switches a deeper or 'thockier' sound" and smooths switches without making them feel sluggish. MakeUseOf says lube "can help lessen" the loud tapping of linear switches and make them feel much smoother than scratchy unlubed ones. Its author also writes that lubing the upper housing "didn't do much to the sound" and that sound tests cannot fully represent the real world.

Miller-Stephenson lists claims for 3203 on its product page: elimination of scratching sound, elimination of spring jitter, and a new keyboard that feels "broken in". The page gives no test data for them. Kinetic Labs and Miller-Stephenson sell the products they write about.

These reports rest on their authors' impressions, with no measured comparison of a lubed switch against the same switch unlubed. A trial on two or three switches shows whether the feel suits you. petitdoigt plays key sounds recorded from 13 real switches, which you pick from a dropdown in its settings, and its drills are the place to type a few minutes on the result.

What is touch typing?

Touch typing is typing by feel. Your eyes stay on the screen while your fingers find the keys from memory, each finger responsible for its own small group of keys. Between keystrokes every finger returns to a resting spot on the middle row of letters, the home row. Because all ten fingers share the work, the method is also called 10 finger typing, and blind typing is another name. Its opposite is hunt and peck: you look down, find the key and press it with one or two fingers.

The technique dates from the typewriter era. Frank McGurrin, a court stenographer from Salt Lake City, reportedly invented home row touch typing in 1888, and in 1889 Bates Torrey coined the phrase “writing by touch.”

Where do your fingers go when touch typing?

Your fingers rest on the home row. On a US QWERTY keyboard the left hand covers A S D F, the right hand covers J K L ;, and both thumbs hover over the space bar. Slide your index fingers across the keys and you will feel a small bump on F and on J. Those bumps let you find home without looking.

From home, each finger reaches up and down its own slanted column. The index fingers carry the most keys, because each one also covers the column next to it in the middle of the board.

How to touch type: a step-by-step plan

Learn the keyboard in layers: the home row first, then one finger's reaches at a time, then real words. Each step should feel easy before you start the next.

  1. Set up your hands. Sit with your forearms roughly level with the keyboard. Curve your fingers and let them rest lightly on the home row, index fingers on the bumps, wrists floating just above the desk while you type.
  2. Type the home row only. Start with letter groups such as asdf jkl;, then real words made only of home row letters: add, ask, fall, glass, flask, salad. G and H belong to the index fingers, one key in toward the center.
  3. Add the index finger reaches. Bring in R T V B on the left and Y U N M on the right. After each reach the finger goes straight back to its bump, so every reach starts from a known spot. The anchors drill trains that return directly: taps for the fingers you choose, with a return to the home row between them.
  4. Add the middle, ring and pinky fingers, in that order. Middle fingers take E C and I ,. Ring fingers take W X and O .. Pinkies take Q Z and P / along with Shift and Enter. The weakest fingers come last and need the most repetition; when they lag, use these typing exercises for the ring and pinky fingers.
  5. Keep your eyes on the screen. A glance down feels faster in the moment, and it keeps the finger memory from forming. Lay a cloth over your hands if you must. A better crutch is an on-screen keyboard: petitdoigt colors every key by the finger that owns it and lights up the finger to use, so the answer to “where is P?” sits on the screen. No browser can see which finger you actually press with, so that part stays on your honor. The guide on how to type without looking at the keyboard has more ways to break the habit.
  6. Slow down until you are accurate. Pick a pace at which nearly every key lands on the first try. A wrong reach repeated over and over becomes a habit like any other, and in the 136-million-keystroke dataset described below, the faster typists made fewer errors. Our guide to typing accuracy explains what rate to aim for.
  7. Move to words and sentences. Once every letter has its finger, practice with common words and then whole sentences so your hands learn the letter pairs that real text repeats. In English the four most frequent pairs are th, he, in and er.
  8. Add the number row. Each finger reaches two rows up its own column: 1 for the left pinky, 3 for the left middle finger, 8 for the right middle finger, 0 for the right pinky. The reach is long, so drill digits on their own, in groups such as 1990 or 2048, before you mix them into text.
  9. Practice a little every day and use the method for real typing. Short daily sessions teach a keyboard in fewer hours than long ones, as the next section shows. Your everyday email, chat and documents count as practice too, and they keep the new habit in use between drills.

How long does it take to learn touch typing?

Plan on about 35 hours of practice to learn where every key is, spread over several weeks, and more time after that to pass your old speed. Read & Spell, which sells a typing course, puts the best case at 40 WPM in two weeks.

The 35-hour figure comes from a 1978 British Post Office study by A. D. Baddeley and D. J. A. Longman. Postmen learned to type alphanumeric postcodes on a standard typewriter keyboard, in sessions of one or two hours, once or twice a day. The group that trained one hour a day learned the whole keyboard in about 35 hours of training on average. The group that trained two 2-hour sessions a day needed about 50 hours, and its fastest learner was slower than the slowest learner of the one-hour group. Spacing costs calendar time: some members of the one-hour group resented spending twelve weeks learning what colleagues learned in three. They still typed faster and more accurately after 60 hours than the two-session group did after 80, and the authors concluded that keyboard training should be spread over time where possible. Retested one, three or nine months later, the postmen had lost about 30% of their speed, a good reason to keep typing the new way every day.

Expect a dip as well. At first your speed falls below your hunt-and-peck speed, which is normal, and with consistent practice the drop usually lasts no more than a few months. Use milestones instead of dates to decide when to move on:

Is touch typing faster than hunt and peck?

Touch typing gives a small average speed edge, and its larger payoff is where your eyes go. Two studies led by Aalto University researchers measured both.

The larger one logged 136 million keystrokes from 168,960 people who took an online typing test (Dhakal, Feit, Kristensson and Oulasvirta, CHI 2018). Average speed was 51.56 words per minute; our guide to average typing speed puts that number in context. People who reported taking a typing course typed about 5 WPM faster than people who had not, a small gap next to the spread inside each group (a standard deviation of about 20 WPM). Finger count tracked speed more clearly: the fastest tenth of typists, above about 78 WPM, reported using 8.4 fingers on average, and the slowest tenth, below about 26 WPM, reported 5.3.

A motion-capture study explains the pattern. Anna Feit, Daryl Weir and Antti Oulasvirta tracked the hands and eyes of 30 everyday typists who ranged from 34 to 79 WPM (CHI 2016). Self-taught typists used 6.2 fingers on average against 8.5 for touch typists, and the two groups typed at similar speed and accuracy. Three habits predicted speed whatever the technique: pressing each letter with the same finger every time, moving toward the next key before the current one is done, and keeping the hands still over the keyboard. Gaze set the groups apart. Self-taught typists spent about 41% of the time looking at the keyboard, touch typists about 20%, and the authors note that self-taught methods may be worse in tasks that need attention on the display.

Touch typing trains those habits on purpose: one finger per key gives the consistent mapping, and the home row keeps the hands anchored. The fast self-taught typists in the study picked them up through gaming, chatting and programming, and none of them said they practiced typing deliberately. The authors add that touch typists who want to reach and keep speeds above 70 WPM need some form of deliberate practice.

Why do people give up on touch typing, and how do you stick with it?

Most people give up during the dip, the stretch when the old method is still faster. A 2003 study in Human Factors by Yechiam, Erev, Yehene and Gopher starts from that observation: succeeding in a touch typing course does not ensure people keep touch typing in their everyday work. The authors blame melioration, the pull toward whatever pays off right now, which for a typist means looking at the keys. In their experiment, 22 students finished a touch typing course and then typed their own homework. A side task that made them respond to signals on the screen reduced the slide back to looking and helped the skill last.

You can build the same pressure into your own routine:

  • Pick a switch date and touch type everything after it, chat included. Every message you type the old way keeps the old habit alive.
  • Make looking down useless. Cover your hands, or practice where the screen shows you the keyboard, so the information you need is up where your eyes already are.
  • Check your anchor. If sad comes out as dsf, your left hand has slid one key to the right. Gamers often park the left hand on W A S D, one key left of home, so feel for the bump on F before you start.
  • Give the pinkies their keys. Reaching for A or P with the ring finger feels easier, and it breaks the one-finger-per-key mapping that the research links to speed.
  • Use both Shift keys. Holding the left Shift for every capital forces awkward stretches on the left side of the board and twists the hand away from home.

The Dvorak keyboard moves the letters English uses most onto the home row. Your left hand rests on the vowels A O E U, your right hand on H T N S, and each index finger reaches inward for one more home-row letter: I on the left, D on the right.

What is the Dvorak keyboard layout?

The Dvorak layout, officially the Dvorak Simplified Keyboard, is an alternative arrangement of the letter keys designed for typing English. August Dvorak, an educational psychologist at the University of Washington, built it with his brother-in-law William Dealey. They filed for a patent in May 1932 and received US patent 2,040,248, titled simply “Typewriter keyboard”, on May 12, 1936. ANSI recognized Dvorak as an alternative standard layout in 1982.

The design follows a handful of rules. The most frequent letters and letter pairs go on the home row, where the fingers rest, and the rarest go to the bottom row. Vowels sit on the left and the common consonants on the right, so most syllables bounce from one hand to the other and the right hand does a little more of the work. Dvorak also tried to avoid letter pairs typed by neighboring fingers, and to make strokes flow from the edges of the board toward the middle.

The home row alone spells many of the commonest English words. You can type the, and, that, this and into without leaving it. On QWERTY, the alone sends two fingers up to the top row.

A few changes catch QWERTY typists off guard:

  • Punctuation. The comma and period move to the top row under the left ring and middle fingers, the apostrophe sits above A, and the hyphen moves to the key right of S.
  • Shortcuts. C lands where QWERTY has I, V where it has the period, and Z on its slash key. Copy, paste and undo move to the right hand, which gets awkward when that hand holds the mouse. The Colemak FAQ lists this among its complaints about Dvorak.
  • The right pinky. It covers L, S, Z, the hyphen, the slash and the equals sign. The Colemak FAQ also points out that L and S, two frequent letters, often follow each other on that one finger.
  • Brackets. [ and ] move to the right end of the number row, where QWERTY keeps minus and equals.

Is Dvorak faster than QWERTY?

The evidence is too thin to say. The two studies everyone quotes date from 1944 and 1956, and each was run by someone who had already taken sides.

The two classic Dvorak vs QWERTY studies
StudyWhat it foundWhy it is disputed
US Navy, 194414 typists retrained on Dvorak for 83 hours went from 32 to 56 net words per minute, a 75% gain. 18 QWERTY typists given extra training gained 28%.Earle Strong reported that Dvorak, who held the patent, ran the test himself. Liebowitz and Margolis found that the way the QWERTY starting scores were averaged cut that group's measured gain almost in half.
US General Services Administration (Earle Strong), 195610 government typists needed well over 25 days of four-hour sessions to get back to their old QWERTY speed. With further training, a matched QWERTY group improved faster than the Dvorak group. Strong recommended more QWERTY training.Strong had spoken publicly against new keyboard designs before the study, and critics say it was biased in favor of the QWERTY group.

The economists Stan Liebowitz and Stephen Margolis reread both reports for their 1990 paper “The Fable of the Keys”. In a 1996 article for Reason they called the Navy study even worse than Dvorak's earlier experiments and concluded that the story of Dvorak's superiority is “a myth or, perhaps more properly, a hoax.”

Computer models can measure the geometry without human typists. Carpalx, a typing-effort model built by Martin Krzywinski, scores layouts on English text and rates Dvorak's typing effort about 25% lower than QWERTY's. Its breakdown shows where the savings come from and what they cost:

Dvorak keeps your fingers on the home row about twice as often and alternates hands more. It pays for that with nearly twice the pinky work, and it tilts the load toward the right hand about as much as QWERTY tilts it toward the left. The old studies are too partisan to say how many words per minute that smoother motion buys a trained typist. The GSA typists spent more than 100 hours just getting back to where they started, so if speed is your only goal, better technique on the layout you already know pays off sooner. Should you switch to Dvorak?

Dvorak suits you if you type mostly English on your own computers and can live with a few months of slower typing while you relearn. Four situations tip the choice:

  • You don't touch type yet. You will learn finger positions from scratch either way, so Dvorak costs you no unlearning. The method in the touch typing guide works the same on any layout.
  • You write mostly in another language. Dvorak was optimized for English letter frequencies, and other languages have their own frequent letters and letter pairs. Check that language's own layouts first in the keyboard layouts guide.
  • You live on keyboard shortcuts. On a Mac, the “Dvorak – QWERTY ⌘” layout switches to QWERTY while you hold Command, so shortcuts stay where your hand expects them. On Windows, people get the same effect with AutoHotkey scripts.
  • You type on other people's computers often. Every shared machine means changing a setting or typing QWERTY, so plan to keep both layouts in your hands.

If shortcuts and the right pinky are your main worries, look at Colemak, which leaves Z X C V where QWERTY has them. The Colemak vs Dvorak comparison puts the two side by side, and the Colemak guide covers learning it.

How do you switch your computer or phone to Dvorak?

Windows, macOS, ChromeOS, Android, iOS and most Linux distributions include Dvorak, so switching means picking it in the settings. Your keycaps will keep showing QWERTY letters.

Windows 11

  1. Open Start › Settings › Time & language › Language & region.
  2. Next to English in your language list, select the three dots, then Language options.
  3. Next to Installed keyboards, select Add a keyboard and choose United States-Dvorak.
  4. Press Windows + Space to cycle between Dvorak and your usual layout.

macOS

  1. Open Apple menu › System Settings › Keyboard.
  2. Under Text Input, click Edit, then the Add button.
  3. Search for “Dvorak”, select Dvorak or Dvorak – QWERTY ⌘, click Add, then Done.
  4. Switch layouts from the Input menu in the menu bar.

iPhone and Android

Since iOS 16 (2022), iPhones offer Dvorak for English under Settings › General › Keyboard › Keyboards › English. Android's built-in keyboard has supported Dvorak since version 4.1.

One-handed Dvorak

August Dvorak designed left-handed and right-handed layouts in the 1960s for people who type with one hand. Windows includes them as United States-Dvorak for left hand and for right hand, and macOS as Dvorak – Left-Handed and Dvorak – Right-Handed, added the same way as the standard layout.

How long does it take to learn Dvorak?

Plan on roughly 100 hours of practice before a fluent QWERTY typist is back to their old speed. In the 1956 GSA study, ten government typists needed well over 25 days of four-hour sessions, so more than 100 hours, to catch up with their old QWERTY speed. In Dvorak's own 1944 Navy study, typists reached 56 net words per minute after 83 hours of training. At an hour a day, either figure means about three months.

The Navy group started at 32 words per minute, below the Navy's own standard of competence, so its 83 hours say little about how long a fluent typist needs. Short daily sessions aimed at your slowest keys and letter pairs make the most of those hours, and the steps below are built around them.

How to learn Dvorak, step by step

Learn Dvorak as if you had never typed: start on the home row and earn accuracy before you chase speed.

  1. Try it before you change any setting. Open Dvorak in petitdoigt, a free trainer that runs in your browser. It applies the layout itself from the physical key positions, so your computer stays on QWERTY, and the on-screen keyboard shows the Dvorak letters with each key in its finger's color. A short session tells you whether the home row feels right before you commit.
  2. Learn the eight resting keys. Left hand on A O E U, right hand on H T N S. Add I and D with the index fingers, then practice words made only from these ten letters: hand, notes, idea, student.
  3. Drill the fingers Dvorak works harder. Your right pinky carries L and S, and your left pinky handles A, the apostrophe and the semicolon. The anchors drill isolates the fingers you choose and brings them back to the home row between taps. Finger focus tilts the practice text toward those fingers' letters.
  4. Add the top and bottom rows through real words. Bring in P Y F G C R L above the home row and the rare letters below it, first in common words, then in full sentences.
  5. Train your slowest letter pairs. Every pair of letters becomes a new motion on Dvorak. In adaptive mode, the pairs drill measures your key-to-key transitions as you type and builds each set mostly from your slowest and most error-prone ones.
  6. Put accuracy ahead of speed. Every repeated mistake is practice for the wrong motion. Slow down until errors are rare; typing accuracy explains how to measure them.
  7. Keep your eyes on the screen. Your keycaps still show QWERTY, so every glance at them misleads you. Use the on-screen keyboard instead, and see how to type without looking for more ways to break the habit.
  8. Plan the switch. If your job depends on typing, keep QWERTY at work and practice Dvorak in short daily sessions until its speed catches up, then change the system setting for good. petitdoigt keeps separate stats for each layout, so your Dvorak numbers start fresh and your QWERTY history stays intact.

A browser cannot see which finger pressed a key, so the trainer shows the right finger and measures keys and timing, and using that finger is up to you. The keyboard finger chart explains the finger zones, which stay the same when you change layouts.

A German keyboard keeps the QWERTY letter order almost intact, so a QWERTY typist can read one at a glance and still miss when reaching for Z, @ or a bracket. The tables below compare the Windows German layout with US QWERTY key by key and show which finger takes each moved key.

QWERTZ vs QWERTY: what is the difference?

QWERTZ and QWERTY differ in two letters and in most of the keys around the edge of the letter block. Z and Y trade places. The keys that type [, ;, ' and - on a US keyboard carry ü, ö, ä and ß on a German one, and the symbols they pushed out moved to the number row or behind AltGr, which replaces the right Alt key. The shifted number row changes as well. On QWERTZ, Shift+2 types ", Shift+3 types § and Shift+7 types the slash. The parentheses sit one key to the left of where a US typist expects them, on 8 and 9, and Shift+0 gives =.

Everything that no longer fits on the first two levels lives on AltGr: ², ³, braces and square brackets, the backslash, @, |, µ, ~ and €. The left Alt key does not reach them, although Ctrl+Alt works as a stand-in for AltGr in many programs.

Why are Z and Y swapped on a German keyboard?

The usual explanation is frequency: German uses Z all the time and Y almost never. In letter counts of German text, Z makes up about 1.13% of letters and Y about 0.04%, roughly 29 Zs for every Y. English runs the other way, with Y at 1.97% and Z at 0.07%. German Y turns up almost only in loanwords such as Typ or System, so it went to the first letter key of the bottom row, a pinky key. Z moved up to the top row, where an index finger reaches it.

The English Wikipedia lists two more possible reasons and gives no source for any of the three. T and Z often stand side by side in German (jetzt, Platz, Satz), and with Z in the top row the two letters fall to different hands, which would have cut down on jammed typebars. The other is the syllable zu, whose letters became neighbors. The German Wikipedia adds that a ten-finger typist presses Z and U with the same finger, so zu may be slower to type.

German typewriter keyboards were standardized in DIN 2112, first issued in 1928. The PC layout used in Germany and Austria today is based on the October 1988 edition of DIN 2137-2. The 2012 edition of DIN 2137 names it T1, the basic layout, and an extended layout called E1 followed in 2018 and was revised in 2023. QWERTZ also spread to countries that were once part of Austria-Hungary or had strong German ties and used German typewriters, which is why the standard Czech, Slovak, Hungarian, Slovenian and Croatian layouts are QWERTZ as well, each with its own accented letters.

Where are the umlauts, ß and @ on a QWERTZ keyboard?

On a German keyboard, ü sits right of P, ö right of L, ä right of ö, and ß right of 0. Each is a single key, and Shift gives the capital. The symbols moved too, several of them behind AltGr.

The accent keys ^, ´ and ` are dead keys: they type nothing until you press the next key. ´ then E gives é, and ^ then O gives ô. When the next letter takes no accent, many systems print the accent and then the letter, so pressing ´ for the apostrophe in it's gives it´s. The apostrophe lives on Shift+#.

Macs ship their own German layouts. With the input source called German, @ is Option+L; with German Standard it is Option+Q, the same key as on Windows.

Which finger types each key on QWERTZ?

Each finger covers the same physical keys on QWERTZ as on QWERTY, because touch typing assigns fingers by position on the board. The home row reads A S D F under the left hand and J K L Ö under the right, so your right pinky rests on a letter instead of a semicolon.

The right pinky picks up the heaviest new load. Ä, ö, ü and ß together make up about 2.3% of the letters in German text, and all four fall to that one finger, on top of Enter, Backspace and the hyphen. On a US keyboard the same keys type punctuation, so a QWERTY typist who starts writing German hands the right pinky letters on keys where it used to type punctuation. Z goes the other way, from a pinky key to the right index finger.

The pair tz, as in jetzt, alternates hands: left index, then right index. The pair zu stays on the right index for both letters, and it sits in common words like zu, zum and zurück. If you are learning QWERTZ, practice zu, über, schön, Mädchen and Straße until the right pinky stops hesitating.

How is Swiss QWERTZ different from German QWERTZ?

Swiss QWERTZ keeps the German letter rows, drops ß, and uses Shift on the umlaut keys for French accents: Shift+ü types è, Shift+ö types é and Shift+ä types à. It is defined by the Swiss standard SN 074021:1999 and was designed from the start for several languages. Swiss German and Swiss French share the same keycaps; the system setting decides whether the umlauts or the French accents come without Shift.

Does QWERTZ change keyboard shortcuts?

QWERTZ changes every shortcut built on Z, Y or a moved symbol. In most Windows programs a shortcut follows the letter the key types, so Undo (Ctrl+Z) uses the Z key in the top row and Redo (Ctrl+Y) moves down to the first letter of the bottom row. On QWERTY the left hand does Undo alone. On QWERTZ it takes the left pinky on Ctrl plus a stretch to the middle of the top row, or the other hand.

Games mostly carry over, because W, A, S and D keep their places on QWERTZ; only bindings on Z or Y can land somewhere unexpected. Symbol shortcuts cause more trouble. The slash is Shift+7 and brackets need AltGr, so a shortcut documented as Ctrl+/ may need a different key or a custom binding, depending on the program. If you write code all day and rarely type an umlaut, running a US layout on the same keyboard is a reasonable choice, and switching takes one shortcut.

How do you switch between QWERTZ and QWERTY?

You switch between QWERTZ and QWERTY in your operating system's settings. The layout is a software setting, so the same keyboard types QWERTZ or QWERTY whatever its keycaps say, and you can install both and flip between them with a shortcut.

On Windows 11:

  1. Open Start, then Settings, Time & language and Language & region.
  2. Select the three dots next to your language, then Language options.
  3. Under Keyboards, select Add a keyboard and pick the layout you want, such as German.
  4. Hold the Windows key and press Space to cycle through your installed layouts, or pick one from the input language icon in the taskbar.

On a Mac:

  1. Open the Apple menu, then System Settings and Keyboard.
  2. Under Text Input, click Edit, then the add button, and search for German.
  3. Pick an input source and click Add.
  4. Press Control+Space to return to the last input source you used, or set the Globe or fn key to change input source.

German QWERTZ expects an ISO keyboard, which has an extra key between left Shift and Y for <, > and |. A US keyboard follows the ANSI shape and has no key there, so the German layout on a US board leaves those characters without a key. ISO vs ANSI keyboards explains how to tell which one you have.

After a switch, your fingers already know where most keys are, so the errors tend to land on Z, Y and the right-pinky letters.

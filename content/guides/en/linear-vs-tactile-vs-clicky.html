Linear, tactile and clicky describe how a key feels while it travels. A linear switch presses smoothly, a tactile switch has a bump you feel as the key registers, and a clicky switch adds a click to the bump. The three are the main branches of the keyboard switch family, and makers sell most switch lines in all three feels. Published specs from Cherry, Gateron and Kailh show how close they are in force and travel.

What is the difference between linear, tactile and clicky switches?

The difference is in the force curve and the sound. Linear gives smooth travel with no bump or click, tactile gives a bump the typist can feel on actuation with no click, and clicky adds an audible click. On a clicky Cherry switch a click collar fires downward past the actuation point and the slider pulls it back on release. Silent versions of linear and tactile switches add a damper to quiet the press. The petitdoigt switch database sorts its 1,162 switches into 682 linear, 241 tactile, 96 clicky and 143 silent ones.

How do linear, tactile and clicky switches compare on real specs?

Within one maker's line, the three feels sit within a few grams of each other. Force and travel decide how heavy a switch feels, and the feel type changes how the force arrives. Cherry states force in cN and Gateron and Kailh in gf; the two units differ by about 2 percent.

The clicky switch has the highest actuation force in each trio, and the bump peak sits 5 to 25 units above the actuation force on the Cherry and Kailh models (10 on the Cherry Brown, 5 on the Kailh Brown, 25 on the Jade). The Gateron listing gives no bump figure. The same order holds across the database: the median clicky switch states 52 gf, 6 to 7 gf above the median linear or tactile switch.

A bump peak above the actuation force means the force climbs to the bump and then drops. Where makers list the bottom-out force, the Cherry MX2A Red reaches 100 cN and the Brown 80 cN, so the linear switch ends its stroke at the heavier figure. Which is better for gaming: linear or tactile?

No source ranks one feel above the other for gaming. Actuation force, pre-travel and personal preference decide. Linear switches have no bump in the stroke, and makers sell shallow linears for speed. Cherry's MX2A Speed Silver actuates at 45 cN after 1.2 mm, against 2 mm for the standard Red, and bottoms out at 3.4 mm of total travel. Gateron's G Pro Silver lists the same 1.2 mm pre-travel and 3.4 mm total.

Tactile switches give players a felt actuation point. Cherry's Red and Brown share the same 45 cN actuation force, so the bump is the main difference between them. Players who want to set actuation depth precisely look at Hall effect switches and optical switches, which come in linear and tactile versions too.

Which is better for typing: linear, tactile or clicky?

None has been shown to make people type faster or more accurately, so the choice rests on feel and on who shares your room. Tactile switches give your fingertip a bump at the actuation point. Linear switches give a uniform press from top to bottom. Clicky switches add sound, and the click is audible in an office or a call. For noise, compare quiet switches: Cherry's MX2A Silent Red keeps the Red's 45 cN force and moves the actuation point to 1.9 mm of the 3.7 mm stroke.

Because the three feels are close in force, the practical test is to press them. Are Red, Brown and Blue switches the same on every brand?

Cherry set the color convention and other makers copied it, so the feel type carries across brands while the numbers vary. A Gateron G Pro 3.0 Brown lists 55 gf against 45 cN for the Cherry MX2A Brown, and Kailh's Box Brown lists 45 gf. Treat the color as a hint of the feel and read the maker's spec sheet for the force. Brands also invent their own names, such as Gateron's Oil King or Kailh's Box Jade, so check the type on the product page.

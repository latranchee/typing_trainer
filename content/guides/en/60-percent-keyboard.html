A 60 percent keyboard keeps the alphanumeric block of a full-size board and nothing else: the letters, the number row and the modifiers such as Shift, Control and Alt. The function row, the arrows, the navigation keys and the number pad are gone, and many 60% keyboards reach them through a second layer behind a Fn key. The letters stay exactly where a touch typist expects them. The petitdoigt keyboard database lists 79 boards at 60%.

What is a 60 percent keyboard?

It is the smallest common size that keeps a standard typing area. The Deskthority wiki calls the 60% keyboard "probably the best-known size by name" and describes it as one that "comprises only the alphanumeric area of the standard keyboard": 61 keys on ANSI, 59 percent of a full-size board. Corsair lists what it keeps as "alphanumeric keys (number row and letters) and modifiers" and says that "omitting arrow keys, numpad, and function row makes it a very small form-factor keyboard."

How do arrow keys and F-keys work on a 60% keyboard?

They live on a layer: hold Fn and other keys send arrows, F-keys or navigation keys. Corsair gives the example of its K70 PRO MINI Wireless, where "holding down the right FN key turns WASD into arrow keys," and adds that such shortcuts are "never going to be as easy or ergonomic" as dedicated keys.

The QMK firmware shows how a layer works underneath. Its documentation defines the Fn key as MO(_FL), which "moves to the _FL layer while that key is being held down." A keymap can hold up to 32 layers, and a transparent key on an upper layer lets the press "fall through to lower layers," so you only redefine the keys you need. On boards that run QMK or VIA you can remap that layer yourself; boards with proprietary firmware use the maker's app.

What is the HHKB layout, and how does it differ from a standard 60%?

The HHKB layout is a 60-key arrangement designed for Unix work, with Control where most boards put Caps Lock. The Happy Hacking Keyboard was co-developed by the computer scientist Eiiti Wada and PFU Limited and introduced on 20 December 1996, according to Wikipedia, which says its design was "optimized for use in Unix environments." HHKB's own page for the Professional HYBRID Type-S calls it a "Near-Symmetrical 60-Key Modified US ANSI Layout" and puts the Control key "to the left of [A]."

The other changes sit on the right. Wikipedia describes Delete directly above Enter, with Backspace on Fn plus Delete and a DIP switch that swaps the two; the Deskthority wiki credits the Fn key's position with giving "access to Function and arrow keys." The original 1996 model used rubber dome switches, and the Professional range moved to Topre electrostatic capacitive switches in 2003, covered in the guide to Topre switches.

The database holds six HHKB boards, all with 60 keys. The HHKB Studio is the first with mechanical switches, in hot-swap sockets that take MX 3-pin or 5-pin switches, and it adds a pointing stick. PC Gamer scored the Professional HYBRID Type-S 90/100 and noted that the layout takes adjusting to; Tom's Hardware gave the Snow edition 3.5/5 and called it expensive and niche. Four boards from other brands offer an HHKB-style layout option: the KBDfans Agar, the Tofu60 3.0, the IQUNIX TILLY 60 and the Vortex M0110.

60% vs 65% vs 75%: which size should you choose?

Choose by the keys you press without thinking. A 65% board is "a 60% keyboard with dedicated arrow keys," in Deskthority's words, and a 75% board adds the function row back on top.

Who is a 60% keyboard for?

Reviewers point to FPS players and to people who carry their keyboard. Corsair recommends the 60% "for the gamers out there that mainly play FPS games" and says it suits people who "write for a living" less well when they rely on arrow keys. RTINGS found the ROYAL KLUDGE RK61 easy to carry around, adding that some people may not like the missing arrows.

The database shows the gaming lean. Of the 79 boards at 60%, 35 have adjustable actuation, the feature of Hall effect and other analog switches that lets you set where each key triggers, and 33 offer Rapid Trigger. That 44 percent share of adjustable actuation is the highest of any size in the database; the Hall effect switches guide explains the technology. Only 4 of the 79 are low profile.

For touch typing, the home row, the number row and the modifiers sit where they sit on a full-size board. The guide to touch typing covers that block, and petitdoigt's anchors drill trains each finger from its home key on the keys every 60% board keeps.

What are the best 60% keyboards?

Switch type, connection, hot-swap sockets and layout decide it. RTINGS's list of the best 60% keyboards, updated in October 2026, names four:

  • Wooting 60HE v2, its best 60% board, for "exceptionally low latency," magnetic switches with settable actuation and reset points, configuration software "among the best in the industry" and a four-year warranty. It is wired only.
  • NuPhy Air60 HE, its mid-range pick: magnetic switches in a low-profile body, wired only, with a one-year warranty.
  • MonsGeek FUN60 Ultra, its budget pick, with an aluminum case, optional wireless and performance that "comes close to matching the Wooting 60HE v2." Its TMR version also accepts mechanical switches.
  • ROYAL KLUDGE RK61, its cheap pick, with hot-swap sockets and a wireless version but "much higher latency" than the others.

The database gives the wider field. Of the 79 boards, 30 connect wirelessly, 42 have hot-swap sockets of some kind and 65 list a price, from $29.99 for the MonsGeek FUN60 Pro to $559 for the Angry Miao Retro 63 RT, with a median of $119.99. Wooting also sells the 60HE+ Module, a bare board in the GH60 format without case, switches or keycaps. To search the list in your own words, type something like "wireless 60% with hot-swap under $150" into the keyboard finder; the database holds 11 boards that fit that description, including the Keychron V4 Max and the MonsGeek FUN60 Ultra.

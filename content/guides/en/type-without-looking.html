Typing without looking at the keyboard is called touch typing, and some people call it blind typing. Each finger gets a fixed group of keys and finds them from a resting spot on the middle row, so your eyes can stay on the text. The opposite method, finding each key by sight and pressing it with one or two fingers, is called hunt and peck.

Even trained typists glance down. A 2016 study at Aalto University recorded 30 everyday typists with motion capture and eye tracking. The touch typists still spent about 20% of their typing time looking at the keyboard, and the self-taught typists spent about 40%. Keep your eyes on the screen and save your glances for the symbols you rarely type.

How to type without looking at the keyboard in 7 steps

  1. Find the home row by touch. Rest your left fingers on A S D F and your right fingers on J K L ;, with your thumbs over the space bar. Feel for the small bumps on F and J with your index fingers. They tell you your hands are home without a glance.
  2. Give every key to one finger. Each finger covers a short slanted column above and below its home key. Learn the standard assignment and keep it, so the same key gets the same finger every time.
  3. Hide the keycaps or your hands. A towel over your hands or a box over the keyboard takes away the option of checking. As long as you can see the letters, your eyes will keep going back to them.
  4. Look at an on-screen keyboard when you forget a key. A keyboard on the screen sits next to the text, so the glance is short and you keep your place. A good one shows which finger to use as well as where the key is.
  5. Type slowly enough to stay accurate. Correct keystrokes teach your fingers their routes. When you rush and miss, your eyes drop to see what happened, and the old habit returns. If you do peek, type the same word again with your eyes up.
  6. Drill the keys that make you look. Notice which keys pull your eyes down, and check the long reaches first: the number row and the outer columns under the ring finger and pinky. Give those keys short, focused sessions, such as these exercises for the ring finger and pinky.
  7. Practice a little every day, then move to real text. Start with the home row and common words. Add sentences once you stop hunting for letters, then type your own emails and notes with the cover still on.

Why is it so hard to stop looking at the keyboard?

Looking is hard to quit because your eyes are doing a job your fingers have not learned yet. If you hit E with your middle finger on one word and your index on the next, no finger knows that key well enough to find it alone, so your eyes guide it. The 2016 motion-capture study found that the faster typists pressed each key more consistently with the same finger, among the self-taught typists as well as the trained ones.

You also need less memorizing than you might expect. In an experiment at Vanderbilt University, 100 typists who averaged 72 words per minute at 94% accuracy got 80 seconds to fill in the letters on a blank QWERTY keyboard. On average they placed 15 correctly. They typed every letter at speed and still could not say where most of them were. The researchers concluded that typists never memorize the key positions, not even while learning. Practice with the right fingers builds the skill without a mental map of the keyboard.

Your own sense of how you type can mislead you too. A later Vanderbilt study filmed the hands of 48 typists, and 14 of the 24 who called themselves touch typists turned out to use some other finger pattern. In the Aalto study, only 3 of the 13 self-described touch typists followed the textbook finger map perfectly. If you glance down a lot, check your hands against a finger chart before you blame your memory.

How do you find the keys without seeing them?

You find each key by its direction and distance from the home row. A finger rests on its home key, reaches up or down for one keystroke, and comes back. Take the word the: your left index reaches up and to the right from F to T, your right index slides one key left from J to H, and your left middle finger reaches up from D to E. After each reach the finger returns to its home key, so the next reach starts from a known place.

Two habits break this system. Gamers often rest the left hand on W A S D, which puts the index finger on D, one key left of home, and every reach then lands one column off. The other habit is moving the whole hand to each key. In the motion-capture study, slower typists moved their hands around more, while the fast ones kept their hands still and let the fingers travel. Touching F and J before you start and after every pause puts your hands back where every reach begins.

How do you type numbers and symbols without looking?

Numbers work like letters with a longer reach: the finger travels two rows up from its home key and comes straight back, so your right index goes from J up to 7. Typing courses split the number row in more than one way, because its keys sit offset from the letter columns below. A method taught since the 1960s gives both 5 and 6 to the left index, for example. Pick one method and keep it, so each number always gets the same finger.

Capitals and shifted symbols use the Shift key on the opposite hand. For a capital T, your right pinky holds the right Shift while your left index reaches for T. A question mark shares its key with / under the right pinky, so the left pinky holds Shift for it. Each hand keeps its place, and the next reach starts from home. The keyboard finger chart lists every number and symbol with its finger.

Should you cover your hands while typing?

Cover your hands once you know which finger goes where. Checking becomes impossible, so your fingers have to find the keys on their own. Researchers hide the keyboard to test this skill. A 2016 Vanderbilt experiment ran three conditions: the keyboard in full view, stickers over the letters with the hands still visible, and a box over the whole keyboard. Typists who used the standard finger map kept about the same speed and accuracy throughout, while the self-taught typists got worse as they saw less.

Any of these works:

  • A dish towel or scarf draped over your hands. It costs nothing and works on a laptop.
  • A shoebox with the front cut away, set over the keyboard.
  • Blank keycaps or opaque stickers on the letters, which hide the legends and leave your hands visible.
  • A keyboard tray below the desk edge, which puts the keys out of your line of sight.

Wait to cover until you can find the home row and know the finger chart, or you will be guessing. What should you do when you can't find a key?

When you can't find a key, look at a keyboard on the screen instead of the one under your hands. Your eyes stay near the text, so you lose your place less often and the habit of looking down gets no practice. A printed finger chart taped below your monitor does the same job.

The on-screen keyboard in petitdoigt colors every key by the finger that owns it and highlights the finger for the next key, so one glance tells you where to go and which finger to send. A browser only knows which key you pressed, though. It cannot see your hands, so keeping the right finger on the right key is up to you. Correcting by feel counts as practice too. Backspace sits at the far right of the number row, a reach up and out for the right pinky. If you press the wrong key often, slow down until the errors stop; the typing accuracy guide explains how to measure where your errors come from.

Is typing without looking faster?

Typing without looking is sometimes faster, by less than its reputation suggests: of two 2016 studies, one found touch typists faster and the other found no difference. Both found that self-taught typists depend far more on seeing the keyboard.

In the Feit study, the touch typists who spent more time looking at the keyboard were slower, and their speed fell off more steeply with looking than the self-taught typists' did. The self-taught typists kept their speed up while glancing back and forth.

If you already type fast with your own pattern, the reason to switch is attention. With your eyes on the screen you see typos as they appear, and you can read from a document while you type. Once the looking stops, the plan in how to type faster picks up the speed side.

How long does it take to learn to type without looking?

How long it takes depends on how often you practice and how much you have to unlearn. Typing-course sites give estimates from two weeks to reach 40 words per minute, which one site calls the best case, to "under a year" to become a strong typist. Neither figure cites a study.

The order of progress is easier to predict. The home row and the most common letters stop needing your eyes first. The outer columns and the number row take longer, and symbols you type once a week may never fully stick, so a glance for one of those is fine. Your speed will probably drop below your old speed for a while, because each key now waits for a finger that is still learning its route. Short daily sessions are easier to keep up than one long weekend, and they keep the cover habit fresh.

If you aim for speeds above about 70 words per minute, plan on deliberate practice. The authors of the Feit study note that touch typists generally need some form of it to reach and keep that level. Only 3 of their 30 participants said they practiced typing on purpose, and all three were touch typists.

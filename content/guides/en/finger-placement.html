Keyboard finger chart: which finger presses each key?

Each finger presses the keys in its own column: the home row key it rests on, the key above it and the key below it. The rows of a standard keyboard are staggered, a pattern inherited from typewriters: the top row sits a quarter key left of the home row and the bottom row half a key right of it, so every column leans left as it climbs. Your left middle finger rests on D, reaches up and a little left to E, and down and a little right to C. Each index finger owns two columns, and the pinkies take every key out at the edges.

B sits as far from J as from F, so the right index tends to wander over and take it. The chart gives it to the left index, and switching between the two from one word to the next is the habit to break. Y belongs to the right index, which stretches up and left past U to reach it. An ISO keyboard, the kind sold in the UK, has an extra key between the left Shift and Z; it goes to the left pinky too. The guide to ISO and ANSI keyboards shows where the boards differ.

What are the home row keys?

The home row keys are the eight keys where your fingers rest between keystrokes: A S D F for the left hand and J K L ; for the right on a QWERTY keyboard. They sit on the middle letter row. Every reach starts from one of them and ends back on it, which keeps each finger's path short and identical every time you type the same letter. Your thumbs hover over the space bar.

The fingers rest on the same physical keys whatever layout the computer uses, and the letters printed on those keys change from one layout to the next:

How do you find the home row without looking?

Feel for the raised bumps on F and J with your index fingers, then let the other fingers settle on the keys beside them. Keyboards with a numeric keypad often put a bump on its 5 as well. Vintage Macintosh keyboards, from the Macintosh Plus onward, placed their home dots on D and K for the middle fingers; Apple moved them to F and J with its first USB keyboard. Touch the bumps every time your hands come back to the keyboard, and you lose the reason to glance down. The guide to typing without looking at the keyboard builds the rest of that habit.

Which thumb presses the space bar?

Either thumb can press the space bar, so use the one that feels natural and keep using it. The other thumb rests lightly beside it. Both thumbs stay curved over the bar, close enough to press it without moving the hand.

The thumbs also take the keys next to the space bar. On a Mac that puts Command under the thumb, so Command+C is a thumb and a middle finger, with the hand still on the home row.

Which Shift key should you use?

Hold Shift with the pinky of the hand that is not typing the letter. For a capital T, your right pinky holds the right Shift while your left index presses T. For a capital J, your left pinky holds the left Shift and your right index presses J. The typing hand stays on its home keys, so its finger reaches the letter from the usual spot.

Shifted symbols follow the same rule. ! lives on 1, a left-pinky key, so the right pinky takes Shift. ? lives on /, a right-pinky key, so the left pinky does. Grabbing the nearest Shift with the same hand twists that hand off the home row and leaves its next letter to start from the wrong place.

A web page cannot see which finger pressed a key, but it can tell the left Shift from the right, so petitdoigt records which one you used for each capital.

Which finger types the number row?

Typing schools teach the number row three different ways. The number row sits half a key left of the row below it, so each number key straddles two letter columns and could go to either finger. All three methods agree on 1, 5 and 7:

The column method extends each finger's letter column up one more key, following its lean to the left. The shifted method, taught since at least the 1960s, gives each finger the number one place to the right, almost straight above its home key. The mixed method pairs the shifted left hand with the column right hand, so the right index takes only 7 and both index fingers reach toward the middle, which some typists find more comfortable. That makes 6 a right-index key in the column method and a left-index key in the other two.

Pick one method and use it every time. A 2016 Aalto University study recorded 30 typists with motion capture and found three predictors of high performance; the first was that each letter was consistently pressed by the same finger.

Does correct finger placement make you type faster?

Correct finger placement helps by giving every key one fixed finger, the habit the Aalto researchers found in their fastest typists. The number of fingers mattered less. Typists who had never taken a course used about six fingers on average and performed at a similar average speed and accuracy to the course-trained touch typists. Both groups had slow members and fast ones, and the fast ones kept their hands in one position and prepared upcoming keystrokes early. The 30 typists ranged from 34 to 79 words per minute, so the study says little about anyone faster than that.

The touch typing chart builds those habits, because it assigns every key exactly one finger and anchors both hands on the home row. If you already type fast with six fingers and make few errors, the research gives you no reason to start over. Learn the chart if you hunt for keys or your fingers change from one word to the next. The researchers put the cost of reaching their fastest typists' level with touch typing at several hundred hours of deliberate practice. The touch typing guide lays out a learning plan, and how to type faster covers what comes after the chart is automatic.

What are the most common finger placement mistakes?

Avoiding the pinkies

The right pinky owns more keys than any other finger on the chart, and it is the shortest of your four fingers. Typists often hand its work to its neighbors: P with the ring finger, Backspace with the ring or middle finger, Enter with a sweep of the whole hand. Each substitution pulls the hand off its home keys, and the next letter starts from the wrong spot. Drill the pinky keys on their own, slowly, bringing the pinky back to A or ; after each tap.

Typing from the WASD position

If you play PC games, your left hand probably knows W A S D: middle finger on W, ring finger on A, index on D, the position Epic Games describes for Fortnite. That puts the hand one key to the left of the typing home row. Go straight from a game to a chat box and your index lands on D, so it types D where you meant F, and every other left-hand finger is off by one key too. Find the bump on F before you type. Some players move their game controls to E S D F, which Deskthority describes as WASD with the correct finger placement for typing, so the hand never leaves the home row.

Moving the whole hand

Sliding the whole hand toward a key loses the home position, so the next key starts from an unknown spot. It happens most on long reaches: Backspace, Enter and the number row. The Aalto researchers advise moving only your fingers toward the keys and keeping your palms in one place. On a long reach, leave your index finger touching F or J so the hand has a way back.

How do you practice correct finger placement?

Practice finger placement slowly, one finger at a time, until each key goes to its finger without a thought. A short routine:

  1. Rest your fingers on the home row and find the F and J bumps without looking down.
  2. Type slowly enough that every key goes to its own finger. A wrong finger repeated a hundred times becomes the habit you then have to undo.
  3. Keep your eyes on the screen. In the Aalto study, typists without training spent about twice as much time looking at their fingers. When you need to check a key, look at an on-screen keyboard that shows the key and its finger together.
  4. Train your weakest fingers alone, usually the ring fingers and pinkies: tap one of their keys, return to the home key, tap again.
  5. Mix those fingers back into real words once the isolated taps feel easy.

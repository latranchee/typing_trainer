The best keyboard for typing depends on four things: how much force a key needs, how far it travels, how it sounds and how big the board is. No test ranks mechanical switches for typing. The published research measures muscle activity in the lab, and its authors call the effects small, so nothing here is a health claim. The guides to keyboard switches and switch specs explain the terms.

What matters in a keyboard for long typing sessions?

Force and travel are the two numbers the research looks at. Type and sound are the other two.

  • Actuation force is how hard you press before the key registers, given in gf or cN. Cherry's MX2A Red lists 45 cN and its Black 60 cN. Lighter and heavier versions exist for most switch families.
  • Travel is how far the key moves. Standard mechanical switches have about 4 mm of total travel; Cherry's MX Low Profile 2.0 Red has 3.2 mm. The low-profile switches guide covers the short ones.
  • Type is linear (no bump), tactile (a bump at the actuation point) or clicky (a bump plus a click). The linear vs tactile vs clicky guide compares them.
  • Sound matters when other people share the room; see quiet switches.

What does research say about key force, travel and fatigue?

Two lab studies bear on the question, and neither tested typing on mechanical switches the way people use them.

Dennerlein and colleagues (2009) had six participants tap with an index finger on five keyswitch designs while a load cell and fine-wire electrodes recorded force and muscle activity. A higher activation force increased the activity of one hand muscle, the first dorsal interosseous, by 25.9 percent. Longer travel, 3.5 mm against 2.5 mm, decreased muscle activity by up to 40.4 percent in some measures. A buckling-spring switch raised that same muscle's amplitude by 36.6 percent against a rubber dome while shortening how long several muscles were active. The authors conclude that changing a keyswitch's force-displacement characteristics changes the loading on the muscles. The task was finger tapping on isolated switches, not typing, and six people took part.

Coppola and colleagues (2019) compared four micro-travel keyboards from tablets and notebooks, with 0.55 to 1.6 mm of travel, using 20 adults who touch typed and had no history of upper-limb pain. A dome-switch keyboard with 0.55 mm of travel and a higher activation force needed about 12 percent more typing force, drew 6 to 8 percent more forearm muscle activity, produced typing about 8 percent slower and drew roughly twice the discomfort ratings of the others. A second keyboard with the same 0.55 mm of travel and a butterfly switch did not show those results, so the authors concluded that key travel alone does not predict typing force or muscle activity. They describe the differences as small, under 1 percent of maximum voluntary contraction, and note that small effects could matter over a long exposure. Short tasks, self-reported discomfort and unblinded brands limit the result.

Both studies point to force and switch design as variables worth attention. Neither tested a mechanical typing switch of the 45 gf linear, 55 gf tactile or 60 gf clicky kind, so they say nothing about those over a working day.

Linear, tactile or clicky: which do typists pick?

Reviewers praise all three kinds for typing, and the sources do not rank them.

  • Cherry MX2A boards. The Tom's Guide review of the Cherry KC 200 MX calls it a pleasure to type on thanks to its MX2A switches, and Tom's Hardware says the MX2A switches on the Das Keyboard 5QS Mark II type well. Both boards come in tactile and linear versions.
  • Clicky and buckling spring. PC Gamer calls the Unicomp New Model M's buckling-spring typing excellent. The clicky switches guide covers the noise.
  • Low profile. Tom's Hardware credits the Logitech MX Mechanical with a good low-profile typing feel, and the same review says the low height rules out many wrist rests.
  • Topre. TechRadar describes the HHKB Professional Classic Type-S as a small, portable board with Type-S Topre feel. The Topre switches guide has the details.

Cherry's own page for the MX2A Ergo Clear, a light tactile, claims a smoother typing feel and more relaxed typing or gaming over many hours. The page supplies no test data for the claim, and its own numbers differ: 40 cN in the property table, 55 cN in the highlights.

What should you try first?

Start with force. The classic Red and Brown sit in the middle of the range, with lighter and heavier switches above and below them. In the petitdoigt switch database, 702 of the 821 switches with a stated actuation force fall between 35 and 55 gf, and 45 gf is the most common figure, on 225 of them.

A switch tester or a friend's board shows how a given weight feels at the end of a session and what a clicky or tactile bump does to your rhythm. A hot-swappable keyboard lets you change the switches later.

What about the best keyboard for programming?

No source tests switches for programming. RTINGS calls the HHKB Studio good for office and programming work, and lists programming among the weaker points of the Aula F99. The guides to keyboard layouts and ISO vs ANSI cover the layout side.

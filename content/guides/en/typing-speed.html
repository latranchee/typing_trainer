What is the average typing speed?

The average typing speed on a computer keyboard is between 35 and 52 WPM. Where the average lands depends on whose results you count, because every large data set comes from people who chose to sit a typing test.

A 2018 study by Dhakal, Feit, Kristensson and Oulasvirta of Aalto University and the University of Cambridge, Observations on Typing from 136 Million Keystrokes, gives the most detailed numbers. Its 168,960 volunteers each copied 15 English sentences and averaged 51.56 WPM, with a standard deviation of about 20 WPM. The slowest tenth typed under about 26 WPM. The fastest tenth typed over about 78 WPM, and the quickest reached 120 WPM or more.

That sample leans toward keen typists. The volunteers came from the users of a company that sells online typing courses and tests, mostly young people in the US who wanted to test and improve their typing. Their average age was 24.5, and 72% had taken a typing course.

The typing site Ratatype published the results of 506,024 tests taken in April 2026. The median test fell in the 30 to 40 WPM band, and Ratatype says its median user types one word every 1.7 seconds, about 35 WPM. Its data also puts 42.5% of tests under 30 WPM. Ratatype counts tests rather than people and does not say who took them.

If you type 40 WPM, you are faster than about two thirds of Ratatype tests and about 12 WPM slower than the average Aalto volunteer. Typing.com and many other typing sites give roughly 40 WPM as the average WPM without naming a study behind it. That figure falls between the two data sets.

Copying versus writing your own words

Typing tests measure copying. When you compose, you stop to think, and your speed drops well below your test score. In a 1999 study by Karat and colleagues, cited on Wikipedia's words-per-minute page, participants averaged 32.5 WPM when transcribing and 19.0 WPM when composing.

On a phone

Phones are slower. In a 2019 study of 37,370 volunteers led by Kseniia Palin, people typed 36.2 WPM on their phones and left 2.3% of errors uncorrected. With only one or two fingers doing the work, that is about 70% as fast as people type on a desktop keyboard. Over 74% typed with both thumbs, which was faster than typing with one thumb or an index finger.

What is a good typing speed?

A good typing speed for everyday computer work is 40 WPM or more with few errors, and 60 WPM or more is fast. The table sets each speed band against Ratatype's April 2026 results, so you can see how many test takers you outpace.

Speed counts only when the text comes out right. The Aalto volunteers left 1.17% of characters wrong on average, and 90% of them left fewer than 2.66%. Fixing a mistake costs more than the extra keystrokes, because you first have to spot it, so the authors found that cutting errors by a small margin can bring a large gain in speed. If more than 3 characters in every 100 of your finished text are wrong, you leave more errors than nine in ten of those volunteers did. Read the guide to typing accuracy before you push for speed.

What typing speed do jobs require?

US federal clerk-typist and office automation jobs require 40 WPM, under standards set by the Office of Personnel Management (OPM). Those standards cover a few other typing jobs, and court reporters have a certification of their own.

OPM measures words per minute on a five-minute sample with three or fewer errors; at 40 WPM, that is about 200 words. Applicants can pass a test, show a certificate or self-certify, and agencies may test anyone who self-certifies. Results stay valid for three years. Court reporters take the NCRA dictation as steno notes and transcribe them afterward, so those speeds say nothing about speed on a regular keyboard.

Private employers set their own thresholds and test formats. Ask how long the test runs, what kind of text it uses and how it counts errors, then practice under those conditions.

Does typing speed change with age?

Older skilled typists typed as fast as younger ones in a 1984 study by Salthouse in the Journal of Experimental Psychology: General. The study tested typists aged 19 to 72 whose speeds ranged from 17 to 104 net WPM. The older typists tapped and reacted more slowly, yet typed just as fast, possibly because they looked further ahead in the text than the younger typists did.

The Aalto paper does not break desktop speed down by age. Its volunteers averaged 24.5 years old, and three quarters were aged 11 to 30. On phones, Palin's study found that people aged 10 to 19 typed fastest, even though they spent less time on their phones than people aged 20 to 39.

Many typing sites publish WPM-by-age charts; treat any chart that names no study as a guess. For a child or a teenager, this month's score against last month's score tells you more than any age chart.

How do you measure your typing speed accurately?

Measure on your own keyboard, with real sentences, for a few minutes at a time, and read your accuracy alongside your speed. These steps give you a number you can trust and compare later:

  1. Use the keyboard and layout you work on, at your usual desk. A laptop and an external keyboard can give different scores.
  2. Type real prose with capitals and punctuation. Lists of short common words push the score up.
  3. Type for at least three minutes, or five like the federal test. In a one-minute test, a single good or bad sentence moves the result.
  4. Read both numbers, WPM and accuracy. A test that shows net WPM has already subtracted your uncorrected errors.
  5. Take three tests and keep the middle score. Retest every week or two under the same conditions.

Most tests count a word as five characters, spaces and punctuation included, so "I run" counts as one word and "rhinoceros" as two. The words per minute guide walks through the full calculation, gross and net.

What separates fast typists from average ones?

Fast typists make fewer errors, overlap their keystrokes and use more fingers. The Aalto study compared the fastest tenth of its volunteers with the slowest tenth and found differences in four habits:

  • Corrections. The fastest typists pressed Backspace or Delete on 3.4% of keystrokes, against about 9% for the slowest.
  • Fingers. Fast typists reported using 8.4 fingers on average, slow typists 5.3. Almost half of all volunteers (47.6%) said they used nine or ten.
  • Rollover. Most fast typists pressed the next key before releasing the previous one on 40 to 70% of keystrokes. Slow typists almost never did.
  • Letter pairs. Speed on pairs typed with two hands or two different fingers predicted overall speed better than speed on double letters. Slow typists typed double letters quickly and every other pair slowly.

A typing course mattered less: volunteers who had taken one averaged about 5 WPM more than those who had not (54.35 against 49.00), a difference the authors call relatively small.
